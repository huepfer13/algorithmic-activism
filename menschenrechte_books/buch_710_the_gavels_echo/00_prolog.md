# The Gavel's Echo

The tribunal chamber had been a grain storage vault before the siege, and it still smelled of dust and old burlap. Sandbags piled three deep against the eastern windows muffled the light to a gray murk. Above the bench, a single bare bulb swung on its cord each time the distant shelling shook the floor.

Judge Alaric Voss set his pen down at a precise right angle to the case file. He did this seven times a day, and the clerk had learned to count. "We are on the record," Voss said. His voice carried the flat calm of a man who had rehearsed his own death and found it manageable. "Prosecutor Chen, your witness is present. Proceed."

Mara Chen rose without notes. She never carried them; she believed paper made liars of honest people. "The State calls Cora Hale."

Cora Hale walked to the stand with her hands folded, as though entering a church she no longer trusted. She was nineteen, a courier, and her left eyelid twitched in rhythm with the shelling. Simon Pike, the clerk, swore her in. His pen hovered. Simon recorded everything, including the pauses, and he had begun to suspect that the pauses were the only honest testimony the tribunal produced.

"On the night of the eleventh," Chen said, "you carried a written statement through the eastern checkpoint. What did it say?"

"It said the water ration was being diverted," Cora said. "It said people were drinking from the drain."

Defense counsel Eli Navarro did not look up from his chair. "Objection. Relevance to the charge of unlawful assembly."

"Overruled," Voss said. "The charge is speech. The speech is the fact."

Navarro stood then, slowly, and Cora flinched at the scrape of his chair. He had a habit of apologizing with his posture before he attacked with his questions. "Ms. Hale. You knew the emergency decree. You knew the checkpoint was monitored. Why carry it yourself?"

"Because the radio was jammed and the mail was searched," she said. "Somebody had to be the mail."

The floor shook. Dust sifted from the ceiling onto the bench, and Voss brushed it from the file with two fingers, evenly, as if smoothing a grave. Outside, a mortar coughed twice. Every person in the room calculated the distance and decided, separately, to keep sitting.

"Your witness," Chen said.

Navarro leaned on the rail. "One question. If the court finds that your statement endangered the evacuation corridor, will you still say you were right to carry it?"

Cora's twitch stopped. "I'll say I was the only one who carried anything at all."

Voss lifted his pen and held it over the record, and the room waited on the small sound of it touching paper.
