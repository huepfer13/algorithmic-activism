# Abschnitt 2: The Threshold Calibration

The audit notice arrived via the pneumatic tube at 04:47, a crimson sleeve instead of the standard gray. Central Registry. Unscheduled. The platform's chief clerk, a woman named Hala who had not slept in thirty hours, dropped her stylus. Her fingers trembled as she fed the accelerated filing protocol into the docket—seventeen witness statements re-sequenced, three evidentiary exhibits re-tagged, all before the tribunal's morning session. The ventilation cycled louder, as if the platform itself were holding its breath.

Mercer was already at the counsel table when Voss entered. He did not sit down. He stood, one hand flat against the surface, and let the silence stretch until the broadcast feed would have to cut to a wide shot. Same tactic. Different day. Voss noted the amber indicator on the bench flicker—paper dust on the relay contacts, still unresolved from yesterday's session. Kaine had not replaced his glasses. They lay folded beside the audit notice, which he had not yet opened.

"Article Three calibration," Kaine said, his voice rough from disuse. "The tribunal will establish the subject's threshold for life, liberty, and security of person as measurable parameters. Not aspirations. Parameters."

"Parameters require baseline data," Mercer said. He turned to the witness rail. "Sister Odile. You were present at the intake interview. The child's vital signs were recorded at fourteen hundred hours. Respiratory rate, cardiac rhythm, cortisol markers. Were those readings transmitted to the tribunal's calibration engine?"

Sister Odile's hands remained folded in her sleeves. "They were transmitted."

"And the engine's output?"

"A flag. Threshold breach on the security-of-person metric. The engine classified the child's condition as critical."

Petrov shifted at the defense table. His jaw worked. "The engine flags everything critical. It flagged the water pressure in Sector Three last week. A faulty sensor."

"Was the sensor faulty here?" Voss asked. She had not risen. She did not need to.

"The child's cortisol was four hundred percent above baseline," Sister Odile said. "Her heart rate never dropped below one-twenty. She did not sleep for thirty-six hours. The engine did not flag a sensor error. It flagged a human being."

The audit notice on the bench began to vibrate—the tube system pressurizing for the next delivery. Kaine finally unfolded it. His eyes scanned. His jaw tightened.

"Mr. Mercer," Kaine said. "The Central Registry has invoked Article Three, Section Seven. They require the tribunal's calibration methodology for immediate review. All raw data. All algorithmic weights. Within the hour."

Mercer's smile did not reach his eyes. "Then we'd better make the next witness count."
