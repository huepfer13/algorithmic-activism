# The Glass Tribunal

The platform shuddered as the tide shifted beneath it—a slow, mechanical groan that ran through the floor of the hearing chamber and up through the spines of everyone seated there. Judge Aldric Kaine did not look up from the case file. He had learned, in eleven years of presiding over disputes that no shore court would touch, to treat the platform's complaints as background noise. The others had not.

Mara Voss, lead advocate for the detained, watched the ceiling lights flicker and counted the seconds until they steadied. She had been on the platform for six days and had already memorized the rhythm of its failing systems. Seventy-two hours remained on the life-support contract. The tribunal's charter was explicit: verdict before expiration, or the platform would be evacuated and the case would die with the lights.

"The tribunal will come to order," Kaine said, his voice flat as slate. He was a small man with a precise beard and the habit of removing his glasses before delivering a ruling, as though the law required him to see less clearly. "Advocate Voss, you may continue with your witness."

Voss rose. She was tall, deliberate, and had not slept more than four hours since arrival. Her client—a stateless minor whose name the tribunal had sealed—sat behind reinforced glass at the rear of the chamber, visible to the cameras but not to the gallery, which was empty anyway. Every session was broadcast. Every corridor was monitored. There was nowhere to go.

"I call Ilya Petrov," she said.

Petrov entered through the side door, escorted by a marshal. He was a former detention officer, forty-three, with the particular stillness of a man who had spent years watching others and being watched in return. He wore a gray suit that did not fit him. Dane Mercer, counsel for the detaining authority, leaned forward slightly—a tell Voss had catalogued on the first day. Mercer was young, ambitious, and afraid of silence.

"Mr. Petrov," Voss said, "you were stationed at the holding facility on the night of the incident. Describe what you saw."

Petrov's hands rested flat on the rail. "I saw the child refuse the identification scan. Three times. The officer on duty—Officer Brandt—warned her that non-compliance would be treated as a security risk." He paused. "She said she was afraid the scan would find her family."

"Objection," Mercer said, rising. "Hearsay. The witness cannot testify to the child's state of mind."

"Overruled," Kaine said. "The witness is describing what he heard. Continue."

Petrov continued. He described the restraint hold. He described the child's arm. He described the sound the bone made. Sister Odile, seated in the observers' row with her hands folded, closed her eyes. She had been the one to file the initial complaint—a nun who had spent thirty years in refugee camps and had the particular stubbornness of someone who had stopped being surprised by cruelty. Her conflict was simple and intractable: her order had instructed her to withdraw the complaint. She had not.

Voss glanced at the clock. Seventy-one hours and forty minutes. Somewhere below the chamber, the platform's oxygen scrubbers cycled and coughed. The broadcast feed would be running in forty-one languages.

"No further questions," Voss said.

Mercer stood. "Mr. Petrov, did the child strike Officer Brandt before the restraint was applied?"

Petrov hesitated. "She pulled her arm away."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know what you asked."

Kaine removed his glasses.
