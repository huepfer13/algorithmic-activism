# Abschnitt 5: The Arbitrary Threshold

The shudder in the bench had not fully subsided when the intercoms began to whine. A high-frequency shriek bled through the speakers—the damping fields cycling against the new delivery's encryption—and Kaine's hand jerked from the crimson sleeve as if shocked. Voss watched Petrov press both palms against his ears, his chair scraping back.

"Signal interference," Mercer said, not moving from the rail. His voice cut through the whine. "The damping fields are overcompensating. It will pass."

It did, after nine seconds that felt like ninety. The whine decayed into a low hum, then silence. Kaine exhaled through his nose and drew the crimson sleeve open.

"Article Nine calibration," he said. The paper inside was thinner than the dossier pages, almost translucent. "The Registry defines arbitrary detention as any confinement whose procedural justification fails the proportionality test against the subject's documented flight risk. The engine will now ingest the Sector Two confinement logs."

Voss remembered Mercer at the rail when she had entered three sessions past—the same flat-handed stillness, the calibration dossier open to a page he had not turned. He had not moved then either. Now his fingers tightened on the rail's edge.

"The confinement logs were compiled under the platform's emergency protocols," Petrov said. "Those protocols suspended the proportionality review for the duration of the life-support contract."

"Which duration expires in fifty-six hours," Sister Odile said. "The engine does not recognize emergency suspensions. It recognizes parameters. Flight risk assessment at hour twelve. Review hearing at hour twenty-four. The absence of both."

"The protection of life and physical integrity takes precedence," Kaine said. "The Registry encoded that hierarchy. If the confinement logs fail the proportionality test, the engine will flag the detention as arbitrary—and the platform's compliance with the contract will be reclassified as a life-safety violation."

Mercer turned a page. The paper did crisp this time, the air having dried with the damping field's cycle.

"Then the engine will order the platform to release the detainees," he said. "And the platform will refuse."

The intercom whined again—shorter this time, three seconds—and Voss saw Kaine's jaw tighten.

"The engine's verdict arrives in eleven hours," Kaine said. "The platform's life-support contract requires a compliance certification within seventy-two. If the engine flags the detention as arbitrary, the certification cannot be issued. The platform shuts down."

"With everyone aboard," Sister Odile said.

The crimson sleeve lay open on the bench. Kaine's fingers hovered over the paper but did not touch it.
