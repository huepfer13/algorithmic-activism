# Abschnitt 1: The Calibration of Dignity

Mercer did not sit down. He stood at the counsel table, one hand flat against the surface, and let the silence stretch until the broadcast feed would have to cut to a wide shot. Voss had seen him do it before—the pause as weapon, the young advocate's belief that silence could substitute for substance.

"Mr. Petrov," Mercer said at last, "you testified that the child pulled her arm away. Did she also attempt to strike Officer Brandt?"

"I did not see a strike."

"Did you see her raise her hand?"

"I saw her pull away. That is what I saw."

Kaine had not replaced his glasses. They lay on the bench before him, and he studied them as though the answer to the objection were etched in the frames. The pneumatic tube beside the bench hissed—a blocked line, Voss noted, the third time that session—and a fine grit of paper dust sifted down onto the relay contacts. She watched it settle. The platform's maintenance logs would record it. Everything was recorded.

"Mr. Petrov," Mercer pressed, "the tribunal's calibration protocol for Article One requires us to establish the subject's capacity for reasoned conduct at the moment of detention. You are familiar with the protocol."

"I am familiar with the form."

"Then you know that the protocol asks whether the subject acted with reason and conscience. Did the child's refusal of the scan reflect reasoned conduct—or panic?"

Voss was on her feet. "Objection. Counsel is asking the witness to perform the tribunal's calibration analysis for him. The protocol is a judicial instrument, not a witness test."

"Sustained," Kaine said. He picked up his glasses but did not put them on. "Mr. Mercer, you may ask what the witness observed. You may not ask him to rule."

Mercer smiled—the smile of a man who had gotten what he wanted from the exchange regardless. "Withdrawn. Mr. Petrov, when the child pulled her arm away, did she say anything?"

"She said—" Petrov stopped. His hands, still flat on the rail, pressed harder. "She said she was not free. She said it twice. The second time, Officer Brandt told her that freedom was a status to be determined, not a claim to be made."

The chamber's ventilation cycled. Somewhere behind the reinforced glass, the sealed minor shifted in her seat. Sister Odile had opened her eyes again and was watching Petrov with an expression Voss could not read—something between grief and calculation.

"Thank you," Mercer said. "No further questions."

Kaine turned to Voss. "Redirect?"

Voss looked at the clock. Seventy-one hours and twelve minutes. The paper dust on the relay contacts had begun to interfere with the bench's indicator lights; one of them flickered amber, then steadied. She had one question left that mattered, and she had to ask it before the platform's systems—or Mercer's next maneuver—closed the window.

"Mr. Petrov," she said, "when the child said she was not free, did anyone in that facility treat her claim as a request for protection?"

Petrov's jaw tightened. "No."

"What did they treat it as?"

"A malfunction."

Kaine replaced his glasses. The amber light flickered once more.
