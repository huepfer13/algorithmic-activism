# Abschnitt 4: The Calibration of Pain

The drip began at midnight.

Mira had mapped its rhythm by the second hour—one drop every eleven seconds, condensate falling from the thermal drift in the crawlspace above the relay station's switch cabinets. The moisture gathered on the copper conduits, swelled, released. Each impact against the cable housing made a sound like a finger tapping a table, patient and deliberate.

She had not slept. The Ledger's revised parameters lay open on the intake table, and beside it her own calibration logs, the ones Rook had demanded. The pages smelled of ozone and something older, the mineral tang of water that had passed through too many pipes.

Tomas arrived before dawn, his shadow trailing behind him at an angle that did not match the discharge lamps. The fog had taken something from him during the night—she could see it in the way he held his left hand, curled and protective.

"The carriers are being recalibrated," he said. "At the pumping station. Rook's orders."

Mira set down her pencil. The drip counted eleven, twelve, thirteen.

"Recalibrated how?"

"They're calling it integrity verification." Tomas sat on the intake bench. The wood groaned. "Article Five protocols. The Ministry has determined that certain labor arrangements produce—" he stopped, his jaw tightening "—degrading conditions. The carriers must demonstrate they can withstand the verification without physical compromise."

"Withstand what?"

He looked at her. In the lamplight, his eyes held the same translucent quality as Rook's, as if the fog had begun to seep inward.

"Cold," he said. "Isolation. The absence of sound. They're testing thresholds."

Mira thought of the water cart, the wheel that had squeaked three times past the station. The carrier who had counted, who had tried to speak without speaking. The fog would have taken his shadow by now. It would be filed somewhere in the Ministry's lower vaults, a dark shape pressed between translucent pages.

"The auditor's parameters," she said slowly. "They're not about documentation. They're about endurance."

"Pain is a metric now." Tomas opened his hand. A red mark crossed his palm, fresh and precise, the width of a Ledger page. "They calibrated me this morning. A demonstration. To show the carriers what verification looks like."

The drip fell. Eleven seconds. The condensate had begun to pool on the cable housing, a thin sheen of moisture that caught the lamp's flicker and threw it back, distorted.

Cass appeared in the doorway. She carried the tin box—the one Rook did not know she knew about—and her shadow moved independently of her body, lagging behind as if reluctant to follow.

"The Listener sent word," Cass said. "From the lower districts. The carriers are refusing the verification. They've barricaded the pumping station."

Tomas stood. "That's suicide. The fog will take more than shadows."

"Protection of life and physical integrity," Mira said. The words emerged flat, technical, the way one recited calibration constants. "Article Five. The Ministry cannot verify what it has already destroyed."

Cass set the tin box on the table. Inside, Mira knew, were recordings—whispers that had never reached the Ledger, shadows that had been collected without filing. Evidence.

"The audit proceeds at dusk," Cass said. "Rook will expect your logs."

Mira picked up her pencil. The drip counted eleven, twelve, thirteen. Somewhere below, the water cart's wheel began to squeak—once, twice, three times—and then stopped.

"Then we give her logs," Mira said. "Calibrated to a different frequency."
