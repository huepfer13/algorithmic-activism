# The Weight of Unspoken Rain

The Hush came down over Verdal the way it always did, without ceremony, without sound. It rolled in from the sea at dusk, thick and grey as unwashed wool, and where it touched the streets it drank the words from the air. A baker calling the evening price of bread found his voice swallowed mid-syllable; his mouth kept moving, but the fog took what he said and left nothing behind. Only what had been written in the Ledger survived the night. The Ministry of Quiet had made certain of that.

Mira Solano stood at the edge of the lower district with her shadow still attached, which was more than most could claim. She kept a ledger of her own—a small notebook, its pages soft as onion skin—and in it she recorded what the fog ate. *The baker said six coppers. The baker's wife said five.* She wrote in pencil, because ink was rationed and because pencil could be erased, and erasure was a kind of safety. Her particular gift was that she remembered what the Hush took. No one knew why. She did not speak of it.

Tomas Vey had not spoken above a whisper in eleven years. He carried his voice in a tin box strapped beneath his shirt, and when he needed to say something that mattered he opened the box and let the words out one at a time, like coins. The Ministry had confiscated his shadow when he was nineteen; it hung now in the Hall of Quiet Things, a flattened darkness pinned to a board, and sometimes he visited it and pressed his palm to the glass. His conflict was simple: he wanted it back, and wanting was not permitted.

Old Cass ran the water cart in the lower districts, where the drought had cracked the earth into a map of thirst. She had one eye that saw the weather and one that saw the truth, and she never confused them. The Ministry allowed her to sell water because she never asked questions, but she kept a second ledger beneath the cart's false bottom, and in it she wrote the names of everyone who had gone silent.

Ines Rook worked in the Hall of Records, copying the Ledger by hand, and her particular habit was to hum—softly, tunelessly—while she worked. The Ministry had not yet decided whether humming counted as speech. Her conflict was that she had begun to forget her daughter's name, because her daughter had whispered it once without permission, and the fog had taken it, and now no one in Verdal could say it.

The Listener sat at the cliff's edge every evening, where the Hush was thinnest, and listened to what the fog left behind. No one knew where the Listener came from. No one asked.

The threat arrived with the new rationing: beginning next month, the Ministry would collect shadows at random, not only from whisperers. Mira wrote it down. Tomas opened his box. Cass hid her ledger deeper. Ines stopped humming.

And the fog, patient as a creditor, waited for the drought to reach the upper districts.

*Chronicle of the First Dusk: the fog took the baker's price and the baker's wife's answer, and Mira Solano wrote both down, and the writing held.*
