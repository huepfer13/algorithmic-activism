# Abschnitt 5: The Frequency of Absence

The barricade at the pumping station was made of water carts.

Mira counted seven of them from the relay station's upper window, their wheels chocked with stones, their handles lashed together with cable stripped from the municipal lines. The carriers had arranged them in a crescent facing the Ministry's access road, and behind that crescent stood forty-three people whose shadows had begun to thin.

The intercom buzzed. A high-frequency whine cut through the speaker grille—the damping fields Rook had ordered installed at every checkpoint, a sound like wire drawn across teeth. Mira's pencil slipped. The drip counted eleven, twelve, thirteen before she recovered her place in the log.

"Rook is at the lower gate," Tomas said. He had not moved from the intake bench. His left hand remained curled, the red mark on his palm now edged with something that looked like frost. "She's brought the verification team. They have portable isolation chambers."

Cass opened the tin box. Inside, the recordings lay stacked like pressed flowers—whispers captured before the fog could take them, shadows folded into envelopes that bore no official seal.

"The Listener says the carriers will not disperse," Cass said. "They are invoking the integrity clause. Physical protection takes precedence over verification protocols."

"Article Nine," Mira said. The words came flat, technical. "Arbitrary detention. The Ministry cannot hold what it cannot document, and the fog has already claimed their shadows. They are, by the Ledger's own logic, unverifiable."

Tomas looked at her. The frost on his palm had begun to spread, thin white lines tracing the veins.

"The isolation chambers don't require shadows," he said. "They require bodies. Cold. Silence. The absence of sound until the threshold is crossed. Rook calls it calibration. The carriers call it something else."

The intercom whined again. Mira felt it in her teeth—the same frequency as the discharge lamps, forty-seven hertz, the ozone smell sharpening as if Rook herself had stepped into the room. The memory arrived unbidden: the flicker of the lamps at the switch cabinets, the metallic air, the way Rook's shadow had moved before her body did.

"She's testing the barrier," Cass said. "The fog will descend in forty minutes. If the carriers are still outside when it comes—"

"Then their shadows will be confiscated," Mira said. "And filed. And the verification will proceed without them."

She picked up her pencil. The drip counted eleven, twelve, thirteen. On the intake table, her calibration logs lay open to a page she had not written—a column of numbers that corresponded to nothing in the Ledger's parameters, a frequency she had measured in her teeth and never recorded.

"Then we calibrate to a different threshold," she said. "Cass, the recordings. Tomas, your hand. The frost—it's not damage. It's a marker. Rook calibrated you to a frequency the fog can read."

Tomas opened his palm. The white lines had formed a pattern, a sequence of intervals that matched the drip.

"Eleven seconds," he said. "The condensation cycle. She's been broadcasting through the pipes."

Mira stood. The intercom whined. The drip fell.

"Then we answer," she said. "At forty-seven hertz."
