# The Ledger of Small Mercies

The Warden of Weights kept his office above the flour store, where the scales hung from a beam and the ledgers lay chained to the desk. From his window he could see the common square, the pump, and the queue that formed each morning before the bell.

"A closed hand gathers nothing," the clerks said, quoting the drowned founders, and so the ledgers were opened each year and every household rendered its account.

Maren Voss kept the ferry and said little. Her oar was worn thin at the grip, and she knew the river's moods better than the Warden's moods. When the widow Ilse crossed with a basket covered by a cloth, Maren did not ask what the cloth concealed.

"Small mercies are not entered in the book," the clerks said, "and what is not entered cannot be weighed."

Old Tobin mended nets by the landing and remembered the year the water rose. He said: "The founders drowned because they counted the boats and forgot the oars." The clerks wrote his saying in the margin and did not read it aloud.

Sister Ilse kept the almshouse and the widow's secret. "A kindness done in darkness," she said, "is a lamp the tax-man cannot see." The Warden's clerk wrote that down also, and underlined the word *cannot*.

Petr Kral traded in salt and in rumours. He had a crooked thumb and a straight memory. "The scale that never tips," he said, "is the scale that never weighs." He said it in the square, and the queue heard him.

The Warden of Weights came down at noon with the ledger under his arm. He wore the chain of his office and the face of a man who had already decided. "The widow's account shows no bread given," he said, "and yet the almshouse shows bread received. One of these ledgers lies."

The square was quiet. The pump dripped. Maren Voss set her oar against the wall and said: "The river does not ask who owns the rain."

The Warden opened his mouth to answer, and the bell rang for the afternoon queue.
