# The Ledger of Unspoken Things

I was born in the space between two words, and I have spent my life learning that this is the only inheritance the Order cannot tax.

Vellum rises from the cliff face like a staircase built for giants who never came—terraced cells stacked seven storeys above the sea, each one narrow enough that a person must turn sideways to pass another. The salt eats the railings. The wind carries away everything you might have meant to keep. And every dawn, before the light ration is distributed, every citizen must stand at the mouth of their cell and speak their grievances aloud to a listening clerk. Not to a person—to a function. The clerk does not respond. The clerk only records.

I am Ivo Marchetti, and I have been a clerk for eleven years. I know the rhythm of a grievance the way a physician knows a pulse: the ones that come too fast are rehearsed, the ones that come too slow are dangerous. I have heard a woman complain about her neighbour's cooking for six hundred consecutive mornings. I have heard a man say nothing but his own name, over and over, until the Registrar's auditors marked him for correction. I recorded all of it. That is my function.

Sister Perpetua runs the eastern archive, where the ledgers are kept. She has a habit of touching the spine of each book before she opens it, as if asking permission, and she believes—genuinely, dangerously—that the Order's charter is a sacred text. We argued once about the founding line: *all voices are equal.* She said it was a promise. I said it was a description of the intake, not the outcome. She has not spoken to me since.

The Registrar oversees the tallies. No one has seen the Registrar's face. Nadia Volk, who repairs the listening tubes, claims she once saw a hand—only a hand—and that the fingers were stained with ink to the second knuckle. Nadia herself is a problem I have not solved: she repairs the instruments that record us, and she has begun, quietly, to keep a ledger of her own. I know because she asked me once, in the stairwell, whether silence could be deposited rather than merely withheld.

Old Tobin lives two cells above mine. He is ninety-three and has not declared a grievance in four years. The Order has fined him everything he owns, which is nothing, and now they are threatening his light ration. He sits on his ledge at dawn and says nothing, and the clerk assigned to him writes *nil* and moves on. He told me once that the sea is the only honest accountant, because it keeps no record.

The threat arrived on a Tuesday, in the form of an audit. The Registrar's office announced a reconciliation of the Ledger—a review of all entries for the past decade, to correct "discrepancies." Nadia says the word means erasure. Perpetua says it means purification. Tobin says nothing, which is his way of saying everything.

I have begun to notice that my own entries are missing from the archive copies. Three years of grievances, gone. I do not know who is removing them. I only know that the Order has begun to decide which voices were ever spoken, and that I am the one who taught it how.
