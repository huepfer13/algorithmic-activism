# The Debtor's Anatomical Hall

The tide came in beneath the Anatomical Hall like a slow confession, and the building listened. It had been a customs warehouse once, when the river still carried honest cargo, and it kept the shape of that former purpose the way a body keeps the shape of an old wound. Its brick flanks rose four storeys above the mudflats, pierced by narrow windows of greenish glass, and from the highest gable a copper weathervane turned with the patience of a thing that had nowhere else to be. Twice each day the water climbed the lower vaults and sealed them, and twice each day it withdrew, and the Hall exhaled its damp breath across the ledger rooms where the clerks worked by lamplight, measuring what could not be given back.

Ilsa Vane had come to the Hall in the third week of the flood season, carrying a letter of appointment and a small case of instruments that had belonged to her mother. She was a registrar of the Reclamation, and she had learned the Doctrine the way one learns a language spoken only in one's own house — without ever being told what it meant. She counted breath against the brass spirometers, blood against the graduated phials, and memory against the long grey columns of the ledgers, and she did not ask why the columns never closed. Her particular habit was to touch the corner of each page before she turned it, as though the paper might be warm, and her particular trouble was that she had begun to notice how often the same names appeared in the margins of debts she had not written.

Corvin Ashe kept the keys and the hours and the silence. He was a tall man with a lantern-burn on one cheek and the manner of one who had long ago decided that questions were a kind of trespass. He unlocked the vaults at the ebb and locked them at the flood, and he never spoke of what the water carried down. Sister Meret came each morning from the almshouse beyond the river, bringing broth and clean linen and a small brass bell she rang before entering any room, so that the confiscated might know she was only a woman and not a seizure. Bram Kettle, the porter, had a voice that had been reassigned to him from a stranger and never sat comfortably in his throat; he sang under his breath when he was afraid, and he was afraid most of the time.

The Registrar — for so the Hall called the one who kept the master ledgers, and no other name had survived the office — sat in the upper chamber where the windows were highest and the light was thinnest, and wrote in a hand so small it seemed to be hiding from the page. It was the Registrar who had signed Ilsa's appointment. It was the Registrar who had, in the same week, entered against her name a debt of three hundred and forty days, of which she had no recollection and no notice.

The threat gathered quietly, as such things do. On the fourteenth night, when the flood had sealed the lower vaults and the lamps guttered low, Ilsa found in the margin of a ledger a debt recorded against a child who had died before the entry was made — and beneath it, in the Registrar's small hand, a second entry reassigning the child's name to a living stranger. She touched the corner of the page. The paper was warm.
