# Abschnitt 3: The Calibration of Servitude

The purge left a fine grey film on every surface, and the overhead lamp above the hatch console stuttered at forty-seven cycles — a flicker that turned Vera's hands into stop-motion ghosts as she traced the intake seam. The ozone bite at the back of her throat sharpened each time the lamp caught, a smell like a shorted wire wrapped in hot plastic.

"Registry's revision isn't a threshold change," Vera said, pulling her fingers away from the seam. "It's a reclassification. They've flagged the four of us as a single decision unit. One voice, one vote — collective liability."

Tobias looked up from the pressure gauge. "That's not a calibration. That's a collar."

"Call it what the paperwork calls it," Vera said. "Article 4, technical annex. No person held in servitude — the registry's language, not mine. They've defined 'servitude' as any configuration where a minority of occupants controls a majority of resources. If we don't file a compliant distribution model before the next audit ping, the hatch stays sealed and the recycler defaults to minimum flow."

Oskar's jaw worked. The foil strip — Mirela still had it wound around her index finger, a thin silver band that caught the stuttering light and threw a hard flicker across his cheek. He blinked. The same blink. The same tired counting behind his eyes.

"Minimum flow means what, exactly?" he asked.

"Four hours of breathable air," Mirela said. "Then the recycler shuts down to protect its own components. It's not cruelty. It's a maintenance schedule."

"Then we file a distribution model that satisfies the definition," Tobias said. "Equal shares. Equal votes. No minority control."

Vera shook her head. "Equal shares of a shrinking pool still produces a minority with less. The audit doesn't measure intent — it measures variance. Any deviation above three percent in consumption triggers the servitude flag."

The lamp flickered again, and the ozone smell thickened. Mirela pressed the foil flat against the console, smoothing it with her thumb.

"Then we calibrate the variance," she said. "Not the shares — the variance. We set a hard floor on individual consumption and a hard ceiling on draw rate. Anyone who falls below the floor gets a mandatory pause in decision-making until their reserves recover. Anyone who exceeds the ceiling gets their access curtailed. It's a physical constraint, not a moral one."

Oskar stared at the foil. "And if someone falls below the floor and can't recover?"

Mirela met his eyes. "Then the pause becomes permanent. And the hatch stays sealed."

The recycler coughed. The lamp steadied. Somewhere in the module's bones, a relay clicked over.

"Motion," Vera said, her voice flat. "Article 4 as variance constraint. Floor at two liters per hour. Ceiling at four. Pause protocol on breach. All in favor?"

"Aye," Mirela said.

"Aye," Tobias said.

Oskar hesitated. The foil's glare still hung in his vision, a thin hard line dividing the room into before and after.

"Aye," he said.

Vera tapped the console. "Motion carries. Three voices."

"Three?" Mirela said.

Vera didn't look up. "The registry flagged four. The audit counts three. Someone's already been reclassified."

The lamp flickered. The ozone smell sharpened. And on the console, the foil strip began to curl at its edges, as if the metal itself were trying to pull away.
