# The Ministry of Sufficient Dignity

The Tower of Proportional Remedies had been designed, in a moment of bureaucratic optimism, to house eleven ministries. It now housed one, and that one had never quite forgiven the building for the promotion. The heating had been condemned in 1987 and had responded to the condemnation by refusing to die, producing instead a low, accusatory warmth that reached the fourth floor on alternate Tuesdays and the fifth floor never. Inspector Vell had learned to read the radiators the way other men read weather, and he did not like the forecast.

Form D-1 was the only form. This was not a simplification but a doctrine: every citizen possessed, by statute, a right to sufficient dignity, and every exercise of that right required documentation, and every documentation required Form D-1, and Form D-1 required, in its Section 9, a declaration that the applicant was not currently exercising any other right. Deputy Marrow had once pointed out that breathing was arguably a right. The memo she received in reply was four pages long and did not contain the word "breathing" once.

Clerk Ostrander guarded the filing system, which predated the electricity and had developed opinions. It sorted by a principle he described as "resonance" and refused to explain. When an applicant's file failed to surface, Ostrander would press his palm to the cabinet and listen, and sometimes the cabinet would sigh, and Ostrander would write down the time of the sigh, because the surveillance clerks on the sixth floor recorded each sigh as a potential procedural violation, and a violation required a hearing, and a hearing required a room, and the room required the elevator.

The elevator held two people, or one person and a filing trolley, or — in the case that had generated the standing order — one person, a trolley, and a grievance, which was not a person but had been treated as one for eleven minutes. Dr. Pemberton, the tower's medical officer, had written the standing order himself and regretted it daily. His particular burden was that he could measure dignity. He had a scale for it. It was not a good scale, but it was calibrated, and the calibration was his, and so every number the tower produced passed through his hands and came out slightly wrong in a way only he could see.

Applicant Quill arrived on a Thursday with a claim so ordinary it was almost rude: she wished to be spoken to without being shouted at, and she wished this in writing, and she wished it within forty-eight hours. She had brought her own pen. She had brought, in fact, three pens, on the theory that the tower consumed them.

What Quill did not know — what no applicant knew, because the knowledge was filed under resonance — was that her claim had already been processed. It had been processed the previous month, by a clerk who no longer worked there, under a reference number that had since been reassigned to a boiler inspection. The tower had therefore already decided her dignity, and had decided it insufficient, and the decision was now standing in the basement waiting to be rediscovered, the way a splinter waits.

Vell read her form twice. On the second reading he noticed the reference number, and he felt the radiators go cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.
