# The Ledger of Ash and Salt

**Prologue: The Third Year of the Quota, Month of Frost**

On the first day of Frost, in the third year of the Quota Regime, I began to count what they told us not to count.

I am Ilse Varnholt, junior ledger-keeper of the Provisions Office, Third Desk, River Gate. My hands are stained with ink that will not wash out, and my days are spent recording what the Regime decrees: so many loaves to the North Quarter, so many candles to the Watch, so many measures of salt to the Ashworks where the condemned labor until their names are struck from the rolls. I write what I am told. I have always written what I am told.

But on the first day of Frost, Brother Anselm came to my desk with his cracked spectacles and his trembling hands, and he asked me to reconcile the grain ledgers of the Cathedral hospice. He is a small man, Brother Anselm, with a habit of counting on his fingers when he thinks no one watches, and a conflict that lives in his throat—he cannot speak a lie without his voice breaking, and so he has learned to speak very little. He showed me the numbers. The hospice received forty measures of grain. The Regime's ledger recorded sixty. Twenty measures had vanished into some clerk's arithmetic, and the children of the hospice had eaten thin soup for a week.

I should have reported the discrepancy. Instead, I opened a second ledger. A private one. Bound in cheap leather, hidden beneath the floorboard of my room above the chandlery.

On the fourth day of Frost, Marek Toth—who drives the salt carts from the river docks and has a scar across his jaw that he claims came from a fall, though everyone knows it came from a guard's baton—told me that the Ashworks received half the salt recorded in the Provisions Office. He counted the sacks himself. He always counts the sacks. It is his peculiarity, and perhaps his only defense against the things he has seen inside those walls. He asked me to write it down. I wrote it down.

On the seventh day of Frost, Sister Ottilie of the Cathedral infirmary came to me with a list of the dead. Four names. Three had been struck from the rolls before they died—their rations cut, their medicines denied, their existence erased while their bodies still drew breath. Sister Ottilie has a stillness about her that unsettles the guards, a way of looking at a man as though she has already measured him for a shroud. She said nothing of justice. She asked only that I remember the names. I remembered them.

On the tenth day of Frost, Corin—who is twelve, or perhaps eleven, who runs messages between the River Gate and the Ashworks because he is small and quick and no one thinks to search a child—brought me a note folded into the shape of a bird. The note said that the Regime's inspectors would audit the Provisions Office on the first day of Thaw. They would compare every ledger against every roll. Any discrepancy would be treated as theft. Theft, in the third year of the Quota, is punished by labor in the Ashworks until the name is struck.

I have hidden my ledger beneath the floorboard. I have hidden it well. But Corin knows. Marek knows. Brother Anselm suspects. And Sister Ottilie—Sister Ottilie knows everything, though she speaks of nothing.

The audit comes in ninety days.

*Entered this day, the tenth of Frost, in the third year of the Quota: the private ledger now holds seven discrepancies, four names, and one child who carries secrets in the shape of paper birds.*
