# Abschnitt 1: The Ledger of Small Debts

The shadow creature did not attack. It waited, and waiting was worse.

Kael felt the cold first in his fingertips, then in the roots of his teeth. He had seen sick men in his village hold their breath the same way, as if stillness might buy them another hour. The creature's edges frayed and re-formed, smoke over coals, and in that shifting Kael noticed something he had not expected: it was missing a hand. Where the left wrist should have been, the smoke simply thinned and drifted, unable to close.

"You count us," Aria said, and her voice, sharp as ever, carried no fear that Kael could hear. "Three of us. You are deciding which to keep."

The creature's eyes brightened. "I am deciding which to *record*."

Elian had knelt already, one palm flat against the humming ground, and his fingers traced the etched symbols as though reading a familiar page. "These aren't directions," he murmured. "They're entries. Names, dates, quantities. This glade is a ledger."

The whispering vine above them tightened its coils around a fallen trunk, and Kael heard in its hiss a sound like paper drawn across stone. He glanced up. The trunk was hollow, and through the hollow ran a thin brass pipe, choked with dust and a wad of sodden pulp. Something had been sent through it once, and something had jammed. The dust on the joints was fine as flour, and it had settled into the seams of every coupling, dulling the metal to the color of old bone.

"Nothing moves through there now," Kael said, half to himself. "Whatever carried word between these trees has been stopped a long while."

The creature turned its head toward him, and the motion was too smooth, like a door on well-oiled hinges. "Word stopped. Names stopped. But the ledger did not." It lifted its one whole hand, and the symbols on the ground rearranged themselves, sliding through the soil like fish under ice. "Every traveler who passes is written. Every traveler who is written is owed. I am the collector of what is owed."

Aria stepped between the creature and Elian, and Kael saw her do it without thought, her shoulder angled like a shield she had not known she carried. "Then write us," she said. "Write us and be done."

"You are already written," said the creature. "That is the difficulty. One of you was written twice."

Elian's hand went still on the ground. "Twice," he repeated, and his voice had the flatness of a man reading a diagnosis he did not want to believe.

The vine whispered louder, and Kael understood at last that it was not warning them of the creature. It was reading to them, slowly and without pause, from a record it had kept far longer than any of them had been alive. Somewhere in that record, one of the three had a second entry, and the second entry was the reason the pipe was blocked, the reason the dust had gathered, the reason the collector had come.

Kael reached out and closed his hand around Aria's wrist, and then around Elian's, and held them both. "Then we go to the ledger," he said, "and we read what was written before we were born."

The creature smiled with a mouth that was not quite a mouth, and the ground beneath them began, very gently, to open.
