# Section 2: The Calibration of Breath

The Warden's cataract eye had not moved from the gauge since the dam shuddered. Bramble remembered the morning's measurement—a hand's width lost, the reservoir smelling of iron and old algae, the lodge poles gnawed white and mean. She pressed her tail against a new fissure, feeling the water's cold pressure seek escape through mud and desperation.

Old Fenwick surfaced beside her, whiskers stiff with current. "The Central Registry sends auditors by moonrise. Their seals bite deep."

A ripple of nervousness passed through the reeds. Field mice abandoned cached seeds, otters paused mid-grooming, herons folded necks into shoulders. Pip darted from her cattail thicket toward the pneumatic tube's rusted mouth. The paper dust there—fine, dry, sharp as crushed bone—had gummed the relay contacts for three moons. No reading had traveled that tube since the gauge last agreed with the Ledger. She clawed at the mechanism, desperate for a second eye, for any witness.

Sable circled above, wings silent. "The rule stands on a crooked leg," she called down. "The measure is not the water. The measure is the rule."

"The rule serves the living," Fenwick muttered, counting notches on his sunken log. Seven for beavers, five for otters, three for herons, a smudge for mice. The numbers still did not match. His gullet tightened around the discrepancy.

The Warden's beak clicked once. "Protocol Three engages. Life and limb before ledger." His voice carried no mercy, only calibration. The Ledger's bark slab warmed against his breastbone, scratch-marks invisible to all but him.

Pip's claws found purchase deep in the tube's throat. The mechanism coughed, ejecting a coil of powdered bark. A fresh reading spiraled upward—unasked, unverified, glowing in the failing light.
