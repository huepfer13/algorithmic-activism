# Abschnitt 4: The Calibration of Silence

The condensation had begun before dawn. Pip felt it first as a cold bead rolling down the relay cabinet's rusted flank, then another, until the drip found its rhythm—a slow, deliberate percussion against the cable shafts below. Each drop marked a measure she could not stop counting, even when she tried.

The auditors had not come at moonrise.

They had come at the hour when the reservoir's surface turned the color of old iron, and they had stood at the dam's edge without speaking, their shadows long and thin as reeds. Three of them. No crests, no markings, only the flat patience of creatures who measured things for a living.

The Warden descended from his roost with the Ledger clutched beneath one wing. His cataract eye caught the light and held it wrong, scattering it.

"The gauge," said the first auditor.

Bramble watched from the water's edge, her tail still. She remembered the bark coil, the fresh reading spiraling upward—unverified, unread. The tube had spoken, and no one had listened.

The Warden's claw trembled as he turned the gauge's face toward them. The needle sat where it had always sat, pinned to its baseline by Protocol Four.

"And the tube?" asked the second auditor.

"Blocked," the Warden said. "Three moons of dust. The reading did not travel."

The third auditor stepped forward. She was smaller than the others, a heron of no particular age, and her voice carried the dry rustle of paper. "We are not here to audit the gauge," she said. "We are here to audit the treatment of those who read it."

Sable's wings tightened against her body. Fenwick's whiskers stilled.

The small auditor turned her head, and her eye found Pip in the reeds. "You counted the lamps," she said. "Forty-seven beats. You counted until the number lodged behind your eyes. Tell us—did the counting ease the fear, or sharpen it?"

Pip's throat closed. The drip from the cabinet punctuated the silence—one, two, three.

"Sharpen," she whispered.

The auditor nodded slowly. "A measure that cannot be questioned becomes a measure that wounds. The protocol that defaults to baseline when measurement fails—" She paused, and the condensation dripped again. "—that protocol is a form of calibration we no longer permit. The body that cannot trust its own reading is a body under duress. And duress is not a valid instrument."

The Warden's beak opened, closed. His claw tightened on the Ledger until the bark slab creaked.

"Effective immediately," the auditor said, "all readings must be verified by two witnesses. No share may be enforced against a creature whose flesh bears the mark of the measure's failure. The tube will be cleared. The gauge will be recalibrated. And the Ledger—" She looked at the ancient heron, and something in her gaze was not unkind. "—the Ledger will be read aloud, each dawn, for all to hear."

The Warden's cataract eye flickered. "The Ledger is not for all."

"No," the auditor agreed. "It never was. That was the error."

She turned to go, then paused. "One more thing. The lamp rhythm—forty-seven beats. That is a distress signal, not a lullaby. Any creature who counts it three times without relief must be brought before the auditors directly. No protocol may override this."

The three disappeared into the reeds, and the drip from the cabinet counted them gone.

Fenwick surfaced slowly, his scarred snout working. "The baseline—"

"Will be measured against flesh now," Sable said. "Not against bark."

Bramble looked at the Warden. He stood motionless, the Ledger still clutched beneath his wing, and for the first time she saw not a tyrant but a creature who had forgotten how to put down what he carried.

The condensation dripped. One. Two. Three.
