# Abschnitt 3: The Calibration of Servitude

The powdered bark coil lay between them like a fallen moon, its glow fading into the ozone-heavy dark. Pip's whiskers twitched at the sharp smell bleeding from the relay cabinet—that acrid bite that meant the discharge lamps were flickering again, their stuttered rhythm painting the reservoir's edge in fits of sickly yellow. Forty-seven beats to the breath, she had counted once, twice, until the number lodged behind her eyes like a splinter.

Bramble hauled herself onto the bank, tail dripping, and pressed her nose to the coil. "The reading's changed. The gauge no longer matches what the Ledger says."

"Then the Ledger lies," Sable said, descending to a low branch. Her voice carried no accusation, only the flat weight of observation.

Old Fenwick surfaced, his fur slicked dark with silt. "The Ledger does not lie. The Ledger *binds*." He turned his scarred snout toward the Warden's roost, where the ancient heron stood motionless against the dying light. "Every creature's share is written. Beaver, otter, heron, mouse. We are numbered, and the numbers hold us."

Pip remembered the whispers in the reeds—how they had shifted pitch in recent days, less fear threading through them, more calculation. She had heard the field mice speak of a different accounting, one where the smudge beside their names grew to something a body could survive on. The memory prickled beneath her skin like a burr.

"The tube was blocked," she said. "Three moons of dust. No reading traveled. So what did the Warden read from, if not the gauge?"

Silence pooled in the hollow. The lamps stuttered—forty-seven, forty-seven—and the ozone smell sharpened until it coated every tongue.

The Warden's beak clicked once, twice. "Protocol Four," he said, and his voice was the sound of a ledger closing. "When measurement fails, the measure defaults to the last verified entry. All shares revert to their registered baseline. No exceptions."

Fenwick's gullet worked. "Baseline means—"

"Means the mice receive their smudge," the Warden said. "Means the otters receive their five. Means the beavers receive their seven. The Ledger does not adjust for want. The Ledger holds."

Bramble felt the fissure behind her widen, water seeping through mud that could no longer hold. "And if the baseline kills us?"

The Warden's cataract eye fixed on her, pale and depthless. "Then the Ledger records the loss. That is its function."

Sable's wings rustled. "The rule stands on a crooked leg," she murmured, but softer now, as if the crookedness had found its way into her bones.

Pip looked at the coil of bark, at the fresh reading spiraling upward, unverified, unread. The tube had spoken. But the Warden would not hear it. The lamps flickered their forty-seven beats, and the ozone stung her nose like a warning she could not name.

Then Fenwick lifted his head. "The auditors arrive by moonrise," he said slowly. "They will ask to see the gauge. They will ask to see the Ledger. And they will ask—" He paused, water dripping from his whiskers. "—they will ask who calibrated the silence between."

The Warden's claw tightened on the Ledger's bark slab. For the first time, Pip saw something move behind his cataract eye—not mercy, not calibration, but the faintest tremor of a creature who had forgotten how to measure anything but fear.
