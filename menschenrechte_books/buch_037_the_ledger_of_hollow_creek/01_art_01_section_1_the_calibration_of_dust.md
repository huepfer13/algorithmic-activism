# Section 1: The Calibration of Dust

The gauge had dropped a hand's width, and the Warden's silence stretched longer than the dry season. By mid-morning the reservoir smelled of iron and old algae, and the beavers' lodge poles showed white where bark had been gnawed twice shorter, twice meaner.

Bramble stood at the water's edge, her flat tail pressing mud into new cracks along the dam's face. Each compression sent a plume of silt through the shallows. She worked without speaking. The Warden watched from his perch on the dead cottonwood, crooked leg locked, one eye clouded by cataract. The Ledger hung against his breastbone, bark slab gray as the sky before rain.

Old Fenwick surfaced near the intake grate, whiskers dripping. He had spent the night counting notches on his sunken log—seven for the beavers' share, five for the otters', three for the herons', and for the mice, a smudge where the reed-roots drank. The numbers did not match the Warden's scratch-marks. Fenwick submerged again, carrying the discrepancy like a stone in his gullet.

Sable landed beside the Warden, her wings folding with deliberate quiet. "The gauge reads lower than your marks," she said. "The water does not lie."

"The gauge reads what the gauge reads," the Warden replied. His beak did not move toward the Ledger. "The measure is not the water. The measure is the rule."

Pip watched from a cattail thicket, nose twitching. The whispers in the reeds had changed pitch—less fear, more calculation. She smelled something else beneath the drought-stink: fine paper dust, dry and sharp, drifting from the old pneumatic tube that once carried gauge-readings to the Warden's perch. The relay contacts had gummed with it, seasons of powdered bark clogging the mechanism. No reading had traveled that tube in three moons. The Warden read by eye alone.

A hiss of pressure escaped the intake grate. The dam shuddered. Bramble's tail slapped once—warning or summons, none could tell.

The Ledger's marks had never been verified. The gauge had never been calibrated against a second eye. The rule stood on a single crooked leg.
