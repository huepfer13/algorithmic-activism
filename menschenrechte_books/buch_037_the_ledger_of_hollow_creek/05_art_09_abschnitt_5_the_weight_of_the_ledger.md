# Abschnitt 5: The Weight of the Ledger

The drip had not stopped. Pip counted it still—forty-seven, forty-eight—though the auditors were three days gone and the cabinet's rust no longer wept. The rhythm had moved inside her, a second pulse that would not be stilled.

The Warden had not descended from his roost since the reading.

From the dam's edge, Bramble watched him through the reeds. The Ledger lay open across his knees, its bark pages curling at the corners, and his cataract eye moved across marks no other creature could parse. Each dawn, he read aloud now—the shares, the rations, the measured allotments—and each dawn, his voice grew thinner, as if the words themselves were drawing water from his throat.

"The tube is clear," Fenwick reported, surfacing with mud on his scarred snout. "Sable and I worked the blockage loose. The reading travels now."

"Then the gauge will move," Bramble said.

"The gauge will move," Fenwick agreed. But neither of them looked toward the Warden's roost.

The intercom crackled—a high, thin whine that made Bramble's teeth ache and her thoughts scatter like minnows before a shadow. She shook her head hard, and the sound subsided to a buzz. The dampening fields had grown stronger since the auditors' visit, as if the valley itself resented being measured.

Pip appeared at the water's edge, her small paws wrapped around a reed. "He reads the same numbers," she said. "Every dawn. The same numbers, but the water falls."

"Because the numbers were never the water," Bramble said.

The Warden's voice carried across the reservoir then, thin and dry as old paper: "Share of the beaver—three measures. Share of the otter—two measures. Share of the heron—" He paused. The pause lengthened. "Share of the heron—one measure."

Sable's wings rustled. "One measure," she repeated. "He has halved his own."

"To show fairness," Fenwick said. "To show the Ledger bends."

But Bramble watched the Warden's claw tremble as he turned the page, and she remembered the auditor's words: *The body that cannot trust its own reading is a body under duress.*

The intercom whined again, sharper now, and Pip flinched. "The fields," she whispered. "They're testing something."

That night, the Warden did not descend for the evening reading. The reservoir lay still, the gauge needle frozen at its new mark, and the silence pooled in the hollow where the lamps stuttered—forty-seven, forty-seven—until the ozone smell coated every tongue.

At dawn, Bramble climbed to the roost.

The Warden sat where he had always sat, the Ledger open on his knees, but his head had fallen forward and his beak hung slack. The pages beneath his claw were blank. Not empty—erased. The marks had been lifted clean, as if the bark itself had forgotten what it carried.

"Fenwick," Bramble called. "Sable. Come quickly."

They gathered at the base of the roost, and Fenwick's whiskers flattened. "The Ledger—"

"Is empty," Bramble said. "And he is not reading."

The intercom screamed—a single, sustained note that drove them all to the ground, paws and wings pressed against their ears. When it stopped, the silence that followed was worse.

Pip spoke first, her voice very small: "If there is no Ledger, who decides the shares?"

Sable looked at the Warden's still form, then at the gauge, then at the dry and cracking mud where the reservoir's edge had been. "No one," she said. "Or everyone."

The question hung in the air like condensation on rusted metal, waiting to fall.
