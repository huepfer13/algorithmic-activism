# The Ledger of Hollow Creek

The dry season came to Hollow Creek not as a thief but as a creditor, patient and exacting, and it called in every debt at once.

By the third moon without rain, the reservoir behind the old dam had shrunk to a brown coin ringed by cracked mud. The beavers had gnawed their lodge poles twice shorter and twice meaner. The otters slid down banks that ended in dust. The herons stood in the shallows like gray sentinels, and the field mice crept to the water's edge only when the reeds hid them.

At the center of it all stood the Warden, an ancient heron whose left leg had healed crooked after a snagging, so that he always leaned, always watched. Around his neck hung the Ledger—a slab of bark bound in gut-string, its marks known to no one but him. Each dawn he read the water gauge, dipped his beak, and scratched a new figure. Each dawn he rationed: three beakfuls to the beavers, two to the otters, one to the herons, and for the mice, whatever the reeds could hold.

"Fair," he croaked, and the word settled over the valley like silt.

Bramble the beaver did not think it fair. She had dammed the creek with her own teeth and her father's before her, and she said so, loudly, at the water's edge. "My tail built this wall. My tail should drink from it."

Old Fenwick the otter said nothing, but he began to count. He counted the Warden's beakfuls and his neighbors', and he kept his count in a notch on a sunken log where no heron could see. Sable the heron, youngest of the flock, watched the Warden's crooked lean and wondered aloud whether a ledger no one could read was a ledger at all. The Warden heard her and said only, "The gauge does not argue. Neither should you."

Pip the field mouse was smallest and hungriest, and she had begun to hear whispers in the reeds—whispers of a fairer accounting, of marks that all could see, of a ledger kept in the open where the sun could read it. She did not know who whispered. She only knew that the whispers made her less afraid, and that frightened her more than the drought.

Then one morning the gauge dropped a full hand's width in a single night, and the Warden's scratch-mark trembled on the bark. He looked at the dam, at the cracked mud, at the faces turned toward him, and for the first time he did not say *fair*. He said nothing at all.

The whispers in the reeds grew louder that evening, and somewhere beneath the shrinking water, the old dam groaned.

*When one hand holds the measure and every mouth goes dry, the thirsty learn to count for themselves.*
