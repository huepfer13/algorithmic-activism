# Abschnitt 3: Flicker in the Lamp Room

**April 7**  
I woke to the sharp ozone tang curling from the switch cabinets, the discharge lamps stuttering at 47 Hz like a dying pulse. Tomas slipped me a folded square of the blue‑inked log paper he’d rescued from the shredder relay; the dust still warm against my palm. He said the gauge had held steady at 3.4 bar for the last hour, but the paper’s texture reminded me of the tag Rook pulled from the water—a white plastic scrap that now sits tucked in my boot, a silent tally of whatever is being fed into the machine. I noted the cistern reading: 31 days left, the number Mira scratched onto the whiteboard yesterday after she erased the 4 and wrote 2. The pipe is blocked again. Third time this week. I told Pavel to keep the net mended; his voice cracked when he said the fish still taste like metal, and I realized we’re measuring more than calories now—we’re measuring the weight of what we’re forced to recycle.

**April 8**  
Mira approached me at dawn, her eyes hollow but her hands steady. She recalibrated the servitude parameter to zero, a silent check we run each morning to ensure no one is being held in any form of bondage, as the technical directive demands. She whispered that the audit team will compare our bodies to the numbers, and that any deviation in the life‑integrity parameter triggers an automatic ration cut. I felt the weight of the white plastic tag press against my thigh as I logged the day’s intake: 2,050 calories, 2.2 liters, zero unauthorized speech. The lamps flickered again, a brief 47 Hz stutter that made the ozone smell sharpen, and for a moment I thought I saw a shadow move behind the relay housing—something that shouldn’t be there.

**April 9**  
Old Pavel sat by the cistern, his hands trembling as he poured the last of the filtered water into his chipped mug. He said the net needed mending again, but his words were slurred, the salt on his lips tasting of rust. I logged his intake as 1,800 calories, 1.9 liters, and noted the servitude parameter still zero. The gauge danced between 3.2 and 3.8 bar, the familiar flicker that has become our heartbeat. I slipped the white plastic tag from my boot onto the table beside the logbook; Rook had not returned from his midnight walk, and the tide had taken another meter overnight, leaving the north marker submerged. I heard the pneumatic tube hiss, a soft warning that the audit notice might arrive sooner than we expect.
