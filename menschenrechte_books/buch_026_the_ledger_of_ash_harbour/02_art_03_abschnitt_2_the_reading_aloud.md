# Abschnitt 2: The Reading Aloud

*Anno 17—, the twelfth day of the Quarantine, as recorded by my hand, Maren Holt, clerk of the third district.*

---

At the seventh hour the runners were searched.

I saw them from the loft window — the boys of nine and ten, their ledgers strapped to their chests as they had been since the tubes failed, now halted at the eastern checkpoint with their coats opened and their papers spread on the guard's table. The guard read each page aloud, slowly, as if the words were foreign to him. The boys stood in the cold with their hands at their sides and their breath rising in small clouds. One of them, the cooper's son, had been running since dawn and his legs shook beneath him. The guard read his ledger twice and then waved him through, and the boy ran on, but slower now, as if the reading had taken something from him.

By the eighth hour the Harbour Office had posted the notice: *By order of the Harbourmaster, all ledgers passing checkpoint are subject to public reading. Corrections Pending to be verified against the central registry before re-entry.*

The central registry. The phrase went through the district like a cold wind. No one had seen the central registry in years. It lived, they said, in a room beneath the Harbour Office, and its keepers were old men who never came to the surface. If your name was not in the central registry, the notice implied, you were not pending correction. You were simply gone.

At the ninth hour I went to the chandlery counter and found Tobias Renn already there, his small precise hands sorting through a stack of permits. He did not look up when I approached.

"The central registry," I said. "When does it arrive?"

"Tomorrow," he said. "Or the day after. The Harbourmaster has sent for it." He set a permit aside and took up another. "There is to be an audit. From the Central Registry itself. They are sending a clerk."

"To read us aloud?"

"To verify." He looked up then, and his eyes were tired. "The paper-slough has made us careless. Names have been entered twice. Names have been struck without petition. The audit will correct this."

"Or it will correct us," I said.

He did not answer. He only took up his pen and made a small mark in the margin of the permit before him — a mark I had seen before, in the *Corrections Pending* column, beside my brother's name and mine.

At noon Sister Cora came to the counter. She did not speak of the notice. She only set down a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth and said, "For the runners. They will be searched again at the third checkpoint. Tell them to carry nothing but what is written."

"What is written," I said, "is being read aloud."

"Then let it be read," she said. "But let it be read by them first." She pressed the bundle into my hands and was gone before I could ask what she meant.

Inside the oilcloth was a copy of the *Corrections Pending* column, in her hand, with the third name still beneath ours. And beneath that, in smaller script: *The central registry does not know the Drowned Bell. It knows only the Harbour Office. If the audit comes, it will not find us.*

At dusk Elias came down from the loft and read the notice twice. "They mean to read us aloud," he said, "and then they mean to strike us."

"Yes," I said.

"Then we must be worth reading."

The candle guttered. In the last light I saw the runners coming back through the eastern gate, their ledgers open in their hands, their faces pale with the reading. The cooper's son was last. He had lost his coat at the second checkpoint, and his ledger was gone.
