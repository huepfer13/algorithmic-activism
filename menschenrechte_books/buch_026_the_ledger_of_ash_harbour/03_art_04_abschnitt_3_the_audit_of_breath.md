# Abschnitt 3: The Audit of Breath

*Anno 17—, the thirteenth day of the Quarantine, as recorded by my hand, Maren Holt, clerk of the third district.*

---

At the sixth hour the lamps in the chandlery began to fail.

They had burned without flicker since the quarantine began — the Harbour Office saw to that, for a dark district is a restless district — but on this morning they stuttered at a rhythm I could count, forty-seven beats to the minute, and each stutter brought a sharp smell of ozone from the switch-boxes behind the counter, as if the wires themselves were burning. Tobias Renn set down his pen and pressed his thumb to the bridge of his nose.

"Forty-seven," he said. "The generator at the east wall is failing. They will not repair it until the audit is done."

"Then the audit will be done in the dark," I said.

"The audit will be done by clerks who carry their own lamps," he said, and took up his pen again.

At the seventh hour the runners came in. The cooper's son was not among them. The others would not say his name. They stood in a row at the counter with their ledgers open and their eyes on the floor, and when I asked after him the eldest only said, "He had nothing written. They struck him at the second checkpoint and sent him to the holding yard."

The holding yard. Where the paper-slough was burned and the names were held for verification. I knew the yard. I had seen it from the loft window — a walled square behind the Harbour Office where the smoke rose all day and the guards walked with their ledgers chained to their wrists.

At the eighth hour I went to the loft and found Elias already there, the *Corrections Pending* column spread before him. My brother's name was still beneath ours. The third name — the one Sister Cora had written — was still there also, in her small careful hand, and beneath it the words she had left: *The central registry does not know the Drowned Bell.*

"The holding yard," Elias said. "They will hold him until the audit. And then they will strike him, because he has no worth in the ledger and no petition to restore it."

"He is nine years old," I said.

"The ledger does not know age," he said. "It knows worth. And worth is written by clerks."

At the ninth hour the audit clerk arrived.

He came through the eastern checkpoint with two guards and a lamp of his own, and he carried the central registry in a case of oiled leather strapped across his chest. He did not speak to the guards. He did not speak to the runners. He walked to the chandlery counter and set the case down and opened it, and the pages inside were not paper but vellum, old and stiff, and the names on them were written in a hand older than any I had seen.

"I am to verify," he said. "Bring me the *Corrections Pending* column and the petitions of two free householders for each name, and I will verify."

"There are no petitions," Tobias said.

"Then the names are struck," the clerk said, and took up his pen.

I thought of the column I had found in the last light of a candle, the column Tobias had written in his small precise hand. I thought of my brother's name in fresh ink. And I thought of the holding yard, and the smoke, and the boy who had lost his coat and his ledger and his name.

"Wait," I said. "I have a petition."

The clerk looked at me. The lamps stuttered. The ozone smell rose sharp and bitter from the switch-boxes, and in the flickering light I saw Tobias Renn's hand move — not to his pen, but to the margin of the permit before him, where a small mark waited beside a name I had not yet read.
