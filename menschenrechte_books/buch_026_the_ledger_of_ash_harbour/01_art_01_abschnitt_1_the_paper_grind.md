# Abschnitt 1: The Paper Grind

*Anno 17—, the eleventh day of the Quarantine, as recorded by my hand, Maren Holt, clerk of the third district.*

---

On the tenth day the pneumatic tubes failed.

I was at the chandlery counter when the Harbour Office's speaking-tube gave its last wheeze and went silent. The brass fitting above the door, which for nine days had spat sealed capsules of permit and denial into the district clerk's basket, now breathed nothing. When the watchman unscrewed the cap, a fine grey powder sifted out onto the counter — paper-slough, the clerks call it, the ground remains of a thousand permits ground down by the friction of their own relays. It settled on the contacts like ash from a fire no one had lit.

"Blocked at the second junction," the watchman said, and wiped his thumb across the brass. The powder left a grey smear. "Renn will have someone's head for it."

By noon the runners had begun. Boys of nine and ten, carrying ledgers strapped to their chests, sprinting the checkpoints between districts because the tubes could not. And with every runner came the delay, and with every delay came the miscount.

I saw it first at the eastern gate. A woman — the widow Voss, who keeps the net-mending stall by the fish stair — held her permit up to the guard. It bore the seal, the number, the half-ration mark. The guard read it twice. "This permit was struck from the ledger yesterday," he said. "The tube says so. Or it would, if the tube worked." He took the paper from her hand and folded it into his coat. "You will wait for correction."

"Correction of what?" she said. "I am written. I am right here."

"You are written in the old hand," the guard said. "The new hand has not yet come through."

She stood at the gate until the bell rang the ninth hour. I watched her from the counter, and I did not go to her, and the stone of my silence grew heavier by one more turn of the screw.

At dusk Elias came down from the loft — reckless, but he could not bear the dark — and read over my shoulder as I copied the ledger's margins. "The paper-slough," he said, tapping the grey dust that had gathered in the seam of my desk. "That is what the city grinds us into. Fine enough to blow through a tube. Fine enough to be forgotten by morning."

"Then we write larger," I said.

We worked until the candle guttered. And in the last light I found it: a column in the ledger I had not seen before, headed in Tobias Renn's small, precise hand — *Corrections Pending: names struck for want of civic worth, to be re-entered only by written petition of two free householders.* Beneath it, in fresh ink, my brother's name. And beneath his, my own.

Sister Cora's knock came at midnight. She did not enter. She only pressed a folded paper through the gap beneath the door and whispered, "The Harbourmaster has ordered the tubes cleared by morning. And the runners searched. Every ledger carried through a checkpoint is to be opened and read aloud." Her footsteps receded down the stair. I unfolded the paper. It was a copy of the *Corrections Pending* column, in her hand, with a third name added beneath ours: *Cora, of the Drowned Bell.*

The candle died. Elias said, from the dark, "They mean to read us aloud, then."

"Yes," I said. "So we must be worth reading."
