# Abschnitt 1: The Calibration of Hands

The shadow creature did not attack. It tilted its head, coal-eyes narrowing, and then it spoke again in that voice of rust and roots.

"You walk as three. But the vine counts four."

Aria opened her mouth to answer, but Kael's hand rose. He had seen it too—the way the symbols on the ground had shifted beneath his boots, rearranging themselves into a pattern that looked less like a path and more like a ledger. Elian knelt and pressed his palm to the earth, and the hum beneath his fingers felt like a mechanism straining against something stuck.

"The old pipe," Elian murmured, brushing a film of pale dust from a seam in the soil. "There is a conduit here. It is blocked. Paper grit in the relays—someone has been feeding documents into the roots."

Aria laughed, sharp and short. "The forest keeps records now?"

"Everything keeps records," the creature said. "The question is who is permitted to read them."

Kael stepped between the creature and his companions without deciding to. His body had chosen before his mind had finished weighing the danger, and he noticed this with the mild surprise of a man watching his own hands work.

"Then read us," he said. "We have come for medicine. For a village that is dying. For verses that will not write themselves. For a son who promised his father he would learn what heals."

The creature regarded him for a long moment. When it moved, it moved like smoke deciding to become a door.

"Three wishes, then. But the vine does not grant. It calibrates." Its gaze drifted to Aria, then to Elian, then settled on Kael. "You will be measured against one another. Each of you will be asked to bind yourself to a choice, and the binding will hold only if it is given freely—by all parties, in full knowledge, without coercion. This is the old law of the glade. It has never been repealed."

Aria's wit sharpened like a blade drawn halfway. "And if one of us withholds consent?"

"Then the conduit stays blocked," the creature said, almost gently, "and the village keeps dying, and the verses keep unwritten, and the father keeps waiting." It gestured with a tendril of smoke toward the pulsing ground. "The mechanism is old. It does not care for your urgency. It cares only that the hands which turn it are willing."

Elian looked at the dust on his fingers—fine, grey, the color of ash from a hearth long cold. Somewhere beneath them, gears were grinding against a jam of forgotten paperwork, and the whole forest seemed to hold its breath around the obstruction.

Kael met the eyes of his companions, one and then the other, and asked the only question that mattered.

"Are we willing?"

The creature smiled, and the shadows around it leaned in to listen.
