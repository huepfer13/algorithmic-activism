# The Ledger of Quiet Rooms

**Letter from Maren Voss to her brother, unsent, dated 14 October**

Dear Anders,

They have given me a room with a window that faces the sea, and I have decided to find this generous. The window does not open. I am told this is for my protection. I am told many things for my protection.

You should know the ferry has stopped. The storm took the pier at low tide and the captain will not risk the crossing again until the water settles, which the Warden says will be six weeks, perhaps longer. We are five guests and the staff and no one is leaving. I write this knowing Sister Odile will read it before you do, if you ever do. She reads everything. She has a way of folding the corner of a page when she has decided a sentence is dangerous, and she does not hide the fold. I think she wants us to know she has been there.

There is a man here named Ilya Brandt who counts the spoons after every meal. He counts them aloud. He says a ledger that is not spoken is a ledger that can be altered. Tobias Renn, who is young and thin and never warm, has begun counting with him, quietly, under his breath, and I do not know if this is solidarity or fear. The Warden walks the corridor at nine and at three and writes in a black book what he hears through the doors. He wrote down that I was singing. I was not singing. I was crying, and I have learned the difference matters to no one but me.

The supplies are counted too. Sister Odile weighs the flour on a scale that belonged to her mother. She writes the weight in her own hand and signs it, and she has told me, without looking up, that she signs it because she will answer for it. I asked her to whom. She did not answer, and I have thought about that silence for three days.

Anders, I am afraid of the quiet more than the storm. In the quiet, every word I say becomes a document, and every document becomes a version of me that I did not choose. I have started writing things down so that at least one version is mine. If this reaches you, do not write back. Write to the Warden instead, and ask him for the weight of the flour. Ask him twice.

Your sister,
Maren
