# The Bell of the Ninth Gate

In the city of seven tiers, where walls rose like the ribs of some great sleeping beast, there lived those who listened and those who were listened to.

At the lowest gate, where the river coughed its last before the stones drank it dry, Mira carried water in a cracked clay jar. She counted each drop as she walked, for the Bell-Keeper's ledger knew her name and the exact measure of her thirst. Her gift was stillness—she could stand so quiet that the listeners hidden in the stone forgot she was there, and so she had learned which walls had ears and which had only dust.

Corin met her at the second threshold, where the gate bell hung silent and patient. He was a smith's son who had broken his hammer arm and could no longer strike iron, so he mended bells instead. His fingers knew the language of bronze, the small fractures where sound leaked out like blood from a wound. "The ninth bell hums," he told her, his voice barely above breath. "I heard it in the night. It should not hum. It has no tongue."

Old Sable sat at the third gate with her begging bowl and her eyes that saw through cloth and flesh alike. She had been a listener once, before they cut the truth from her tongue. Now she traded in silences, selling the spaces between words to those who needed to hide. "The council knows," she whispered to Mira, pressing a dried fig into her palm. "They have doubled the ration. They mean to keep us weak while they decide who drinks and who does not."

Tobin, the youngest of the Bell-Keeper's apprentices, carried the ledger through the upper tiers. He was fourteen and had never seen the river, only the pipes that sang in the walls. His hands trembled when he wrote, for he had begun to notice that the numbers did not match the mouths. Some names appeared twice. Some disappeared entirely. "I am afraid," he confessed to no one, for no one in the upper tiers spoke of fear.

And the Keeper himself, who had rung the seven bells for forty years and never once touched the ninth, sat in his tower and watched the water gauge fall. His ledger was precise. His ledger was complete. And in the margin, in ink that had faded to the color of old blood, someone had written a single line: *When the ninth bell rings, the city drinks or the city dies.*

The humming grew louder on the seventh night.

The listeners in the stone began to write faster, their styluses scratching like rats in the walls.

And at every gate, the people pressed their ears to the bronze and waited for a sound that had been silent for a generation.
