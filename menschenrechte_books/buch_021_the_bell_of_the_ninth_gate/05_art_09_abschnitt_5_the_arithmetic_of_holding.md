# Abschnitt 5: The Arithmetic of Holding

The high, thin whine began without warning—a needle of sound driven through the speaking-tube grille beside the relay panel. Tobin flinched, his screwdriver slipping half a turn, and the glyph on the wall guttered like a candle in a draft. "Interference," he muttered, pressing his thumb against the tube to still it. "The damping fields are bleeding again." For three heartbeats none of them could hold a thought; the whine scattered concentration the way wind scatters ash.

Mira blinked, and in the blink she was climbing again—the shaft narrowing around her shoulders, then opening into the bell chamber where dust motes hung like a suspended sentence. The memory arrived uninvited, sharp as a splinter: the weight of the ledger's margin beneath the Keeper's thumb, the scraped-away name, the sense that she had walked into a room where the air itself kept accounts.

Old Sable lifted her head from the stone. "The cascade is drifting," she said. "Not the bell—the *holding* of it. Something below is pulling."

The Keeper turned a page with a finger that trembled. Beside the water-ration column, a new parameter had surfaced in the ledger's crabbed hand: a boundary-line, faint as a watermark, marking the zone within which a voice could be *detained*—held in the stone's memory for questioning—without writ, without bell, without the council's leave. The listeners had been granted a wider net. The line had been redrawn in the night, and no one had rung for it.

Corin's bound arm had gone cold. "They are holding words now," he said slowly, "the way they hold water. Rationed. And some words they hold and never give back."

Mira pressed her palm flat against the bronze. The metal was warm—too warm—and beneath her hand the hum had changed its question. "Then we hold the bell instead," she said. "Not the words. The strain. We keep it under the ceiling, and while they are busy listening to us, they are not listening to the shaft."

Tobin reset the gain, his jaw tight against the whine that still threaded the air. The glyph on the wall steadied: the clenched hand, the pulsing dot, the pressure held—for now—within the permitted limit.

Then the speaking-tube crackled, and a voice not their own came through it, flat and patient: "Bell-Keeper. The ninth gate's ledger is required for audit. You will surrender the bound volume at the third bell, and you will come alone."

Old Sable looked at the Keeper. The Keeper looked at the page.
