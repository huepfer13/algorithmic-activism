# Abschnitt 1: The Glade of Listening

The creature of shadow and smoke did not attack. It waited, its coal-bright eyes moving from Elian to Aria to Kael and back again, as though it were reading a ledger written somewhere behind their faces. Then it stepped aside, and the underbrush closed behind it like a door that had never been opened.

Kael let out a breath he had been holding since the glade. "It let us pass."

"It did not let us pass," Aria said, her voice low. "It let us be judged. There is a difference, and I do not yet know which one frightens me more."

They walked on. The path beneath their feet was old beyond reckoning, worn smooth by feet that had gone before, and the whispering vine followed them along the trunks, its tendrils tracing shapes that Elian could not quite read. He stopped once to touch a leaf and found it warm, as if the plant itself were thinking.

The forest opened without warning into a hollow ringed by standing stones. At its center stood a table of grey wood, and on the table lay a small brass apparatus, a kind of speaking-tube with a bell mouth, of the sort that carries messages between distant rooms. It was broken. The tube had been pinched shut near its base, and a fine pale dust, like the grit of old paper, lay scattered across the little brass contacts beneath it. Elian brushed the dust away with two fingers and felt the mechanism resist him, stiff and blocked somewhere deeper in.

"Something has been sent through here," he said. "And something has stopped it."

Aria leaned close. "Then the forest keeps a court. And a court that cannot hear a case is no court at all."

Kael had already moved to the far side of the hollow. There, half-swallowed by moss, stood three low seats of stone, set in a triangle facing one another, and above them a fourth stone that was not a seat but a slab, blank and waiting. He set his hand upon it and found it cold.

"Three seats," he said. "One for each of us. And one above, for whatever listens."

Elian did not like the arrangement. He had come for herbs, for the healing knowledge of his father's trade, and this hollow smelled of no plant he knew. Yet the vine's whisper had changed its tone here, softer now, almost patient, as though it had been waiting a long while for someone to sit down.

"We should not sit," Aria said, though she had already taken a step toward the nearest stone. "Sitting is agreeing."

"We are already agreed," Kael said. "We walked here together. The seats only make it plain."

They sat. The moment the third of them touched stone, the whispering stopped, and the silence that followed was worse than any sound, for it was the silence of a room where a judgment is about to be spoken and no one yet knows whose. Elian looked at the blocked tube on the table and understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the forest air, that whatever had jammed it had done so from the inside.

A single tendril of the vine descended from the canopy above the blank slab and hung there, trembling, as if it were about to write.
