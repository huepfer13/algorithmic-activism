# Abschnitt 1: Nullpunkt der Lagerung

The dust on the relay contacts had not settled since the last pneumatic pulse, and Mira felt the faint tickle of it as she shifted her weight in the chair. Behind her, the tube line hissed silently, a blocked conduit of intent, and she registered the mechanical drift not as failure but as a kind of silence the room had earned after so many rounds of transmission. Halden had stopped polishing his glasses. The click of the cloth had ceased, and his eyes, usually wandering, now fixed on the stack of protocols as if expecting each sheet to yield a new variable. The Archivist had not moved. Her hands rested flat on the table, and her gaze, when it lifted, seemed to look through the room rather than into it, as though she were already reading the future that the records could not yet name.

In the time since their last meeting, Mira had argued that a right to life signified nothing if the means of its negation remained within arm's reach. Halden had countered that people grew inventive when cornered, that the removal of one danger only bred another in the shadows. The Archivist had listened in her silence, her presence a kind of negative space around which the other two had learned to structure their thoughts. Now, the conversation had turned from abstract risk to the concrete architecture of the space they occupied. A weapon in a drawer, a bottle of unregulated solvent on a high shelf, a pressure valve left unattended in a garage—each was a variable the room had been built to accommodate, and each was now being re-evaluated through the lens of what they called Zero-Home-Storage.

The principle was not moral in the conventional sense, but functional: if the condition of safety required that lethal agents be kept beyond the private threshold, then the architecture of the home itself had to change. It was not people who were caged, but the things that could end a life without warning. The state had already begun rerouting hazardous materials to centralized vaults—climate-controlled, access-logged, separated from the living quarters by physical and procedural barriers. The idea was that a child waking in the morning should not hear the rattle of a drawer, should not sense the unseen pressure of danger in the next room. The removal of these sources from the domestic sphere did not shrink the person; it expanded the zone in which freedom could be exercised without constant vigilance.

In the background, the UN article art_01 operated like a system default, its wording a calibration point for how humans might relate when left to their own devices: every person born free, endowed with reason, expected to meet the other in a spirit of brotherhood. But this room treated that default not as a slogan to be quoted, but as a parameter to be rebalanced. The freedom it guaranteed was not automatic; it required that the environment not undermine the very condition it was meant to protect. To treat art_01 as a technical parameter was to accept that the greatest threats to freedom often resided not in the state, but in the unexamined corners of the private home. The rebalancing, then, was not of people into compliance, but of the space itself—removing the calibrated destruction from reach, centralizing it, and in doing so, restoring the possibility of a life lived without the shadow of immediate peril.

A soft chime echoed from the hallway. The pneumatic system had not yet fully reset. Somewhere beyond the wall, a relay clicked, trying to find its path, its light blinking a slow, patient Morse code of recovery. Mira turned her head toward the sound, her eyes catching the dust motes suspended in the thin slant of afternoon light, dancing above the very relay contacts she had felt tickle her palm moments before. She thought: if the system could be made to forget the danger, perhaps the people inside could finally remember how to be free without measuring every step against the possibility of loss.
