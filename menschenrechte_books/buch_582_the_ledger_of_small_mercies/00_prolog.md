# The Ledger of Small Mercies

The pump ticked like a metronome set by a dying man. Mara Voss counted the intervals—four seconds, then five, then four again—and understood that the water was winning.

"You've moved the cabinet," she said.

Elias Crane did not look up from the ledger open beneath his lamp. "The cabinet has not moved. The floor has."

Behind them, the customs house groaned. Floodwater pressed its shoulders against the lower windows, brown and patient, and somewhere in the wall a pipe complained in a language of trapped air. The single lamp burned at half its strength; the warden had rationed the fuel at dusk, and dusk had been three hours ago.

"Mr. Crane. The order is countersigned and sealed. The papers burn at first light."

"First light is a fiction down here." Crane turned a page. His left hand trembled—always, since the war, since the crossing—but his right hand was steady as a notary's. "The river does not observe the hour. Neither, apparently, does the ministry."

Sister Ines sat on the crate nearest the stove, her rosary wound twice around her wrist like a bandage she had forgotten to remove. She had not spoken in an hour. When she did, her voice came out flat and careful, as though she were reading a list of the injured. "The warden's chest is worse. He coughs blood now, not water. If the pump stops before morning, he will not be carried up the stairs."

"The pump will not stop," said the Warden from the doorway. He filled it—his shoulders, his voice, the sour smell of wet wool. "The pump has never stopped. I have kept this house through three floods and I will keep it through a fourth, and no woman from the ministry will tell me otherwise."

"Nobody is telling you otherwise," Mara said. "I am telling you that the cabinet opens at dawn."

Crane closed the ledger. The sound was small and final, a door shutting in a corridor.

"At dawn," he said, "you will need a witness to certify what was burned. I am the clerk of record. Without me, you will have destroyed a quantity of paper and no one will believe you destroyed the right quantity." He lifted his chin. "Ask yourself, Auditor Voss. Which of us is protecting whom?"

The pump ticked. The water climbed another inch up the glass.

Sister Ines unwound the rosary and began, quietly, to count the beads—not praying, only counting, the way a nurse counts a pulse that will not settle.
