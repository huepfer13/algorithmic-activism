# The Ledger of Vanishing Rain

In Kaltbrunn, rain fell only on the roofs of the Ministry of Hydration and the seven government towers surrounding it. The water traced silver lines down their slate tiles, gathered in copper gutters, and vanished into cisterns that no citizen had ever seen. Beyond the government quarter, the outer districts lay under a drought so old that children drew pictures of clouds the way their grandparents had drawn dragons—as creatures from stories, not from life.

Ilse kept the city's maps. She was twenty-three and had a habit of licking her thumb before turning a page, which meant her tongue was always dry by noon. Her office sat on the fourth floor of the Cartography Bureau, where she traced the boundaries of districts, the curves of aqueducts, the precise edges of the rain's permitted path. The maps never changed. That was the point of them.

Marek delivered water to the eastern quarter. He had a crooked shoulder from years of carrying canisters and a way of counting under his breath as he walked—one, two, three, four—so that he would not have to think about the faces at each door. He knew which families had stopped asking for more. He knew which ones had stopped asking at all.

Tomas was a clerk in the Ministry of Hydration. He recorded every citizen's daily words in the public ledger, a vast book bound in grey leather that sat in the central square for anyone to read. He had beautiful handwriting and a tremor in his left hand that had grown worse since the winter. He had begun to forget which entries were true.

Nadia sold tea made from dried nettles in the market. She had a laugh that carried three stalls in every direction and a memory that carried every name in the outer districts. When someone was taken to the Ministry for questioning, Nadia knew before their family did.

The Inspector had no name that anyone spoke aloud. He walked the city with a leather satchel and a face that revealed nothing. He measured water levels. He measured words. He wrote his own notes in a script no one else could read, and when he left a district, something in it had always changed.

On the first Tuesday of the dry season, Ilse noticed that the rain above the Ministry had shifted. Not much—a few meters to the west. She checked her instruments twice, then three times. The rain was following a different path. And on her map, in the space where the new path fell, there was a district that had not received water in eleven years.

She did not report it. She went home and licked her thumb and turned the page of a different map, one she had drawn herself, in secret, of a city where rain fell everywhere.

The Inspector arrived at the Cartography Bureau the next morning. He did not speak. He simply placed his satchel on Ilse's desk and opened it, and inside were maps she had never seen—maps of rain paths that had never been permitted, drawn in a hand she recognized as her own.
