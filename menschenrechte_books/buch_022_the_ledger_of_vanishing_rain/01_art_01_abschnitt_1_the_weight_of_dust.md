# Abschnitt 1: The Weight of Dust

Ilse wiped the pad of her thumb across her tongue, the familiar dryness reminding her that the hour had passed noon, and she turned the page of the atlas spread across her desk. The pneumatic tube that usually hissed with the afternoon dispatch from the Ministry of Hydration was silent; a fine film of paper dust clung to the relay contacts, blocking the flow of the thin cylinders that carried requisition slips. She tapped the metal housing, feeling the slight give of the obstruction, and noted how the dust settled in the same pattern as the rain’s recent diversion— a faint westward shift that had left the easternmost alleys dry for three days straight.

She glanced at the sealed window where the rain traced its silver line over the Ministry’s roof, then slipped her hand into the inner pocket of her coat and unfolded the contraband map she had drawn the night before. On it, the rain’s permitted path was a thin blue line that curled around the government quarter like a ribbon; beyond it, she had sketched alternative channels that threaded through the outer districts, each marked with a tiny asterisk where a cistern lay empty. The Inspector’s satchel, resting on the corner of the table, bore the same embossed seal as the Ministry’s ledgers, and inside, she knew, lay a set of calibrated brass rods that measured not only water levels but the pressure of words recorded in the public ledger—a device tuned to the baseline of one dignity unit, a setting derived from the first article of the universal charter that declared all persons born free and equal in dignity and rights. The rods trembled faintly when the air grew thick with unspoken fear, a subtle drift that Tomas, the clerk with the shaking left hand, had once complained made his entries waver.

Ilse traced a finger along the new channel she had drawn, feeling the ink slightly raised where the nib had pressed harder. She imagined the rain following that line, slipping past the blocked tube, slipping into the dry streets, and the dust on the relay contacts lifting as the pressure equalized. A soft click sounded from the tube as a displaced slip of paper jerked free, fluttering down the shaft like a wounded bird. She caught it mid‑air, the words blurred by moisture: “Request for additional quota—Denied.” She smoothed the slip, placed it back into the tube, and watched the dust settle anew, now mixed with a speck of rain‑wet fiber that gleamed like a tiny star.

Outside, the rain’s edge hesitated, then shifted a fraction toward the west, kissing the roof of the abandoned bakery that had not seen water in eleven years. Ilse closed her eyes, licked her thumb once more, and felt the faint taste of iron on her skin, as if the city itself were bleeding into her mouth.

*On this day, the cartographer traced a line that the rain dared to follow.*
