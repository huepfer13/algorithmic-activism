# Abschnitt 3: The Servitude Threshold

Marek lingered by the switchboard where the discharge lamps flickered at a steady 47 Hz, their staccato light casting jagged shadows that made the ozone tang sharp enough to taste. The Inspector’s leather case lay open beside him, the brass rods inside still humming from the morning’s recall—a vibration he had felt through the floorboards as he stacked the night’s protocol sheets, a low thrum that had once signaled fear but now sounded like a warning tuned to a different frequency.

“Article 4 sets the servitude baseline at zero,” Tomas said, sliding a fresh requisition form toward Marek. “Any reading above that triggers a recalibration cycle, regardless of speech‑pressure scores.” He tapped the ledger where the night’s entries were still wet, the ink bleeding slightly where his pen had slipped earlier. The rods in the Inspector’s satchel, he recalled, had begun to hum before dawn, a low vibration that Marek had felt through the floorboards of the registration hall as he stacked the night’s protocol sheets into their binders. The hum was not the usual faint tremor of accumulated fear—it was sharper, more insistent, the sound of instruments straining against a baseline they could no longer hold.

Marek traced the rain’s silver line on the Ministry roof with his fingertip, remembering how it had shifted westward toward the abandoned bakery after Ilse’s contraband map had shown the hidden channels. The water‑ration ledger lay open, its columns filled with numbers that now seemed to measure not just thirst but the quiet pressure of obedience. The Inspector’s voice, flat as ever, cut through the hum: “Verify the speech‑pressure records against the third‑article baseline first; life and physical integrity take precedence. Then run the servitude scan. If the rods register any deviation above zero, flag the citizen for immediate review.”

A sudden spike in the ozone smell made the lamps stutter, and for a heartbeat the rods emitted a tone that rose above the usual hum—a thin, piercing note that seemed to echo from the very walls of the registration hall. Marek’s pen hovered over the ledger, the weight of the unmarked channels pressing against his palm like a stone. He glanced at Tomas, whose left hand still trembled from the earlier blot, and saw the same question reflected in his eyes: what would happen when the rain, redirected by Ilse’s lines, finally reached the parched streets beyond the Ministry’s silver roof?

The Inspector closed her case with a soft click, the double seal catching the flickering light. “Begin the audit,” she ordered, and the room filled with the soft whir of recalibrating gears, the ozone sharp enough to sting the nostrils, and the relentless, matter‑of‑fact fall of rain upon the ministries’ roofs—while the outer districts waited, their cracks whispering of a future yet to be drawn.
