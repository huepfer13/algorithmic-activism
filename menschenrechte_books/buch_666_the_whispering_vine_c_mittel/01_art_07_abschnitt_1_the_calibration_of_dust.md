# Abschnitt 1: The Calibration of Dust

The creature of shadow and smoke did not attack. It waited, and waiting was worse.

Elian felt the pressure first—a fine grit settling against his skin, as though the forest itself were exhaling dust. He looked down at his hands and saw it: pale, papery residue clinging to his knuckles, drifting in slow spirals toward the ground. Aria noticed it too, brushing at her sleeve with irritation.

"Something is blocked," Elian murmured, half to himself. "The air moves, but it moves wrong. Like a pipe choked with silt."

Kael had already turned away from the creature, his attention caught by something stranger—a thin line of brass tubing half-buried in the moss, running from tree to tree like a vein. It hummed faintly, then sputtered, then hummed again. Where the tubing met a rusted joint, the same pale dust had gathered into a thick crust, sealing the seam shut.

"The forest breathes through these," Kael said, kneeling. He scraped at the crust with his thumbnail. "Someone let them clog. Or something did."

The creature shifted, its coal-bright eyes tracking Kael's hand. "You see the dust," it said. "Do you see what the dust decides? Two travelers may stand before the same sealed pipe. One is heard. One is not. The pipe does not ask who is worthy. It asks only whether the seam was cleaned."

Aria laughed, sharp and humorless. "So we are to be judged by plumbing."

"You are to be judged by nothing," the creature replied. "That is the difficulty. The pipe carries every voice equally or it carries none. There is no third arrangement."

Elian pressed his palm flat against the brass. The vibration traveled up his arm, faint and irregular, like a heartbeat with a stutter. He closed his eyes and listened past the creature's voice, past Aria's breathing, past the deep green silence of the glade—and heard it: a whisper trapped inside the tubing, circling, unable to reach its destination.

"Someone is in there," he said. "A voice. Stuck behind the paper-dust."

Kael rose at once, drawing the small knife from his belt. "Then we open it."

The creature did not move to stop him, but its smoke thickened, curling around the three of them like a question. "Open the seam," it said, "and the pressure releases both ways. What is trapped inside may leave. What is outside may enter. Choose the moment with care."

Aria was already crouching beside Kael, her poet's fingers tracing the crusted joint. She found the weakest point, the place where the dust had cracked into a hairline fissure. "Here," she said. "But Kael—if we break this, and the voice inside is not a voice at all—"

"Then we will have let something into the forest that was not there before," Kael finished. He looked at Elian. "Your call. You heard it first."

Elian knelt between them, one hand still on the pipe, the other pressed to the damp earth. The whisper inside the tubing had changed. It was no longer circling. It was pressing, urgent, against the sealed seam—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came a second sound: a child's cough, thin and frightened, echoing from somewhere deep within the brass network.

"There is a life in the pipes," Elian said quietly. "And it is running out of air."

The creature's eyes dimmed, then flared. "Then the seam is not a lock," it said. "It is a wound. Decide quickly whether you are surgeons or thieves."

Kael set the blade against the crust. Aria's hand closed over his wrist. And Elian, listening still, heard the whisper break into words at last—three words, repeated, desperate: *the eastern junction, the eastern junction*—before the dust swallowed the sound entirely.
