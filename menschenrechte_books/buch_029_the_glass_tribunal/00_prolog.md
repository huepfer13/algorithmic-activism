# The Glass Tribunal

The *Meridian* rode the swell like a held breath. Forty days. The hearing clock above the bulkhead had been fixed there by the consortium's own technicians, its red digits counting down in every camera frame. Mara Voss had counted the lenses twice on the first morning: eleven in the hearing room alone, each one polished to a shine that caught the fluorescent glare and threw it back like a dare.

"Composition of the tribunal," Judge Okonkwo said, without looking up from his papers. His pen moved in small, precise strokes; he annotated everything, even the silence. "The respondents will note that this vessel is chartered under the arbitration rules of the Hague, not the hospitality of their shareholders."

"Noted," said Elias Trent, and smiled the smile that had closed a hundred settlements. He wore no tie. That was the tell Mara had learned to watch for — the loosened collar meant he intended to be liked.

At the witness table, Sister Adaeze sat with her hands folded and her back straight, a small wooden cross at her throat and river mud still grey beneath her fingernails. She had refused the offer of a hotel ashore. "The people who drank from that water are not ashore," she had said, and the camera above her had recorded it, and somewhere in a glass tower a lawyer had flagged the timestamp.

"Madam witness." Trent stepped forward, hands open. "You testified that the contamination began in March. Yet your own clinic's ledger shows no admissions until June. Which is it — the water or the ledger that lies?"

"The children came in March," Sister Adaeze said. "They came in with sores. We had no name for it yet. We have a name now."

Piotr Halvorsen, the consortium's hydrologist, sat at the far end of the respondents' bench and did not look at her. He had the habit of pressing his thumb against the edge of the table until the skin went white, and he was doing it now, and the camera nearest him was angled precisely to catch it.

"Objection," Mara said, rising. "Counsel is testing the witness's memory of a ledger the respondents have refused to produce in full."

"Refused?" Trent turned, mild as milk. "We have produced eleven thousand pages."

"Eleven thousand pages of what, Mr. Trent?" Mara let the question hang in the humming air. "The tribunal will note that the respondents control every lens in this room, every transcript, every byte that leaves this hull. The witness's safety depends on a verdict — and the verdict depends on a record they own."

Okonkwo's pen stopped. "Counsel will confine herself to the objection."

"I withdraw the objection," Mara said, "and ask that it be read into the record."

The judge regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere below the waterline, the ship's engines turned over, and the floor trembled, and the red digits above the bulkhead counted down another minute.

"Read it in," Okonkwo said.
