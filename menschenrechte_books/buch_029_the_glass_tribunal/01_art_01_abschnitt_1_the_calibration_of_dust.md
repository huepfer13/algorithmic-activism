# Abschnitt 1: The Calibration of Dust

The pneumatic tube above the clerk's station coughed and fell silent. Mara Voss looked up from her notes. The brass fitting at the relay joint had gone dull with a fine grey powder — paper grit, she realized, the same abrasive dust that had been accumulating on the contacts since the first morning's filings. The clerk tapped the tube twice with his knuckle. Nothing. He shrugged and reached for the manual tray.

"The witness will continue," Okonkwo said. His pen had resumed its small strokes.

Trent did not sit. He walked the length of the witness table, slow, and the eleven lenses tracked him with the patience of machines that never blink. "Sister Adaeze. You said the children came in March. With sores. Did you photograph those sores?"

"I treated them," she said. "I did not photograph them."

"Did you record their names?"

"Their names are in the clinic ledger."

"The ledger that shows no admissions until June." Trent turned to the tribunal. "The respondents submit that the witness's account is a reconstruction — sincere, perhaps, but a reconstruction. The tribunal is asked to weigh testimony against documentation."

Mara stood. "The tribunal is asked to weigh documentation against the calibration of its own instruments. The respondents' hydrologist, Mr. Halvorsen, has been pressing his thumb against that table for six minutes. The camera above him records it. The camera is theirs. The table is theirs. The pressure mark will be gone when the table is wiped, and the recording will be what they choose to keep."

Halvorsen's thumb stopped. He looked at her then — the first time all morning — and his face did something complicated and small.

"Counsel is addressing the witness," Okonkwo said.

"I am addressing the record," Mara said. "The respondents' instrumentation is calibrated to their own specifications. The tribunal's charter requires independent verification of any measurement used in evidence. I request that the tube relay — currently blocked by their own paper stock — be cleared and that all filings pass through a clerk's hand, not a respondent's sensor."

Okonkwo's pen hovered. "The tube is a convenience, Counsel."

"The tube is a chain of custody," Mara said. "It is blocked. The dust is on the contacts. I ask the tribunal to note the blockage and to note who maintains it."

The clerk looked at the judge. The judge looked at the red digits above the bulkhead — thirty-nine days, twenty-two hours, fifty-one minutes — and set his pen down flat.

"Note the blockage," Okonkwo said. "The witness will answer the question put to her."

Sister Adaeze folded her hands tighter. "The children came in March," she said again, and her voice did not rise. "I have their names in my head. I have their faces in my head. The ledger is a book. The book can be lost. I cannot."

Trent smiled his loosened-collar smile. "No further questions."

"Redirect," Mara said, and stepped forward, and the nearest camera swung to catch her shadow on the witness's face.
