# Abschnitt 2: The Calibration of Breath

The clerk was already sealing the morning's transcripts in a grey binder when the satellite phone buzzed against his hip. He read the screen, and his thumb slipped on the seal. "Central Registry," he said, too loud for the room. "They're sending a revision team. Unscheduled."

Okonkwo's pen stopped mid-stroke. Around the cramped hearing deck, three assistants began collating loose pages with a speed that had not been in them all morning. The red digits above the bulkhead held their count — thirty-nine days, twenty-two hours — but the clerk kept glancing at them as though the numbers had personally betrayed him.

Mara let the silence settle, then turned to Halvorsen. "Mr. Halvorsen. You calibrated the river sensors to detect hydrocarbons at four hundred micrograms per litre. Correct?"

"To the consortium's standard," Halvorsen said. His thumb found the table edge again, then withdrew.

"Your standard. Your specification. And the tribunal's charter requires independent verification of any measurement used in evidence." Mara lifted a single sheet. "Here is the verification protocol. It requires a control reading at zero. A blank sample. Did you run a blank before the March readings?"

Halvorsen's jaw worked. "The blank channel was... offline for maintenance."

"For how long?"

"Eleven days."

"And during those eleven days, the sensors reported no contamination above threshold. The river ran clear, according to your instruments. The children came in with sores, according to the clinic. The tribunal must decide which instrument to believe — the one that was maintained, or the one that was breathing."

Trent rose. "Counsel is badgering the witness with a maintenance log."

"Counsel is establishing that the respondents' own calibration procedure failed at precisely the moment the evidence matters." Mara did not look at Trent. She looked at Halvorsen. "Your protocol has a precedence clause, Mr. Halvorsen. When a sensor's integrity cannot be verified, the higher-order protection governs. Not the reading. The person the reading is supposed to protect. Did you invoke that clause?"

Halvorsen was quiet a long moment. "No one invokes that clause," he said. "It shuts the array down."

"It shuts the array down," Mara repeated. "And it protects the river. Which did the consortium choose?"

Okonkwo set his pen down flat — the same flat, deliberate motion from before, the one that had followed the red digits. "The witness will answer."

Halvorsen looked at the cameras. All eleven of them. "We kept the array running," he said.

The clerk's binder snapped shut. Somewhere below deck, the revision team's launch was already bumping against the hull.
