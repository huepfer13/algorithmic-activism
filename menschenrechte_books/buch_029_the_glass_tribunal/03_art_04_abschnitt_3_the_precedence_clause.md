# Abschnitt 3: The Precedence Clause

The revision team arrived in the hearing deck's doorway like inspectors boarding a ship they already owned. Two men in grey suits, one woman with a tablet held before her like a shield. They did not apologize for the interruption. They did not ask permission. The clerk's binder — the same grey binder that had snapped shut when Halvorsen said *we kept the array running* — was already being prised open again, its seal broken, its pages fanned by a thumb that had not been given leave to touch them.

Okonkwo did not rise. His pen lay flat where he had set it. "The tribunal was not notified of this inspection."

"Central Registry exercises its audit function without prior notification," the woman said. "Article four of the arbitration charter. We require the calibration logs for the March readings. All channels. Including the blank."

The overhead lamps stuttered — a rapid flicker at forty-seven hertz, the frequency of a failing ballast — and the air at the switch cabinets carried a sharp ozone bite, the smell of something electrical straining against its insulation. Mara breathed it in and said nothing.

"The blank channel," she said at last, "was offline for eleven days. The witness has testified to this. The logs will confirm it."

"We are aware of the testimony." The woman's tablet glowed. "We are here to determine whether the testimony constitutes a material breach of the respondents' servitude obligations under the charter's technical annex."

Trent was on his feet before the sentence finished. "Objection. The witness is not a servant of this tribunal. He is a calibrator. The annex governs data custodianship, not—"

"The annex governs the custody of evidence," the woman said. "A witness who withholds a maintenance record holds the tribunal's process in service to his employer. The charter prohibits servitude in all its forms. We are not speaking of persons. We are speaking of instruments. The array was kept in service to the consortium. The blank was kept in service to the silence. Which master did the witness serve?"

Mara did not look at the woman. She looked at Halvorsen, whose thumb had found the table edge again and was pressing hard enough to blanch the nail. "Mr. Halvorsen. Your protocol's precedence clause. When integrity fails, protection governs. Not the reading. Not the employer. The river."

"I know the clause," Halvorsen said.

"Did the revision team's audit function exist when you chose to keep the array running?"

"No."

"Then the clause you failed to invoke protected the river. The clause they are now invoking protects the record. Which one is breathing?"

The lamps flickered again. The ozone smell sharpened. Okonkwo's pen lifted, hovered, and did not write.

The woman with the tablet stepped forward. "The tribunal will produce the blank channel's maintenance log within the hour. Or the witness will be held in contempt of the registry's custody."

Okonkwo set his pen down flat. "The tribunal will produce nothing until it has ruled on the objection." He looked at the clerk, whose hands had gone still over the violated binder — the same stillness that had followed the red digits, the same speed that had not been in him all morning now reversed into stone. "Seal the deck. No one leaves. No one transmits."

The clerk reached for the seal. The lamps went out entirely.
