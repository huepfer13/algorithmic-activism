# The Ledger of the Warren

Beneath the crumbled ribs of the old abbey, where moonlight never reached and the roots of dead oaks hung like withered fingers, the warren breathed in shallow sips. Every breath was counted. Every breath was owed.

Old Fenwick kept the Ledger upon a slab of fallen altar stone, his whiskers grey as ash and his eyes clouded but sharp as flint. Each morning he traced the tally with a claw worn smooth by decades of counting: roots in the eastern store, roots in the western store, mouths to feed, breaths permitted. The drought above had sealed the burrows three seasons past, and the Ledger had grown thin in its columns, lean in its margins.

"You may take one root," Fenwick told the queue that gathered at dawn, "and no more. The Ledger permits it."

Sable, sleek and young and hungry in a way that gnawed at her patience, took her root and held it without eating. "The Ledger permits," she murmured to Bramble beside her. "But who permits the Ledger?"

Bramble, whose left ear twitched whenever fear rose in him, glanced toward the altar stone. "Hush. He counts what we cannot."

"Then perhaps he counts wrongly," said Sable, and bit her root in two.

Tussock, broad and slow and kind, gave half his portion to a doe too weak to stand, and Fenwick's claw paused above the stone. "That is not entered," the Keeper said. "What is given freely cannot be tallied."

"Then do not tally it," said Tussock, and went to dig at a wall that had not yielded a root in weeks.

Pip, smallest of the warren, crept each night to the eastern tunnel where the air grew thin and strange, and there she listened. She heard the scratching beyond the sealed earth—something above, patient and persistent, testing the barricades. She told Fenwick, and Fenwick told her the Ledger had no column for scratching, and sent her back to her burrow.

But the scratching grew louder. And the roots grew fewer. And Sable began to whisper in the dark, and the whispers found ears, and the ears found courage, and the courage found a question no Ledger could answer: when the counting kills the counted, who keeps the Keeper?

The warren tightened around its dwindling stores, and above them the drought pressed down like a lid upon a pot.

*When the measure becomes the master, the measured must learn to count themselves.*
