# Abschnitt 1: The Root That Would Not Move

The shadow creature did not attack. It simply stood at the edge of the glade, its coal-bright eyes tracking each of them in turn, and then it stepped backward into the underbrush and was gone, as though the forest had swallowed its own breath.

Kael was the first to move. He knelt where the creature had stood and pressed his palm flat against the soil, and Elian saw his shoulders tighten.

"The ground is warm here," Kael said. "Warmer than it should be."

Aria had already turned her attention elsewhere. She crouched beside a fallen log and ran her fingers along a seam of pale dust that lay on the bark like frost that had forgotten to melt. "Something ground this," she said. "Not weather. Not age. Something with weight and patience."

Elian stepped past them both toward the vine, which had thickened as they approached the clearing's far edge. Its tendrils were no longer the delicate threads he had noted from a distance. They were ropes now, knotted and pale, and they had wrapped themselves around a rusted iron post that rose from the earth at an angle, as though it had been driven there by hands long dead.

He touched the post. A fine grit came away on his fingers, pale as bone meal, and beneath it the metal was scored with a hundred tiny scratches, each one too regular to be chance.

"It's a relay," Elian said, and his voice carried further than he meant it to. "The vine has grown into the mechanism. The tendrils are threading the contacts."

Kael rose and came to stand beside him. "Then it is not whispering to us. It is whispering through something."

Aria joined them, her journal already open, her pen moving. "There is a blockage further along the pipe," she said, without looking up. "I can hear it in the rhythm. The whispers stutter where the line is pinched."

Elian pressed his ear to the iron. The sound was faint but unmistakable: a dry, papery scrape, the complaint of fine grit caught between metal and metal, a mechanism laboring under a burden it had not been built to bear. Someone had owned this post once, and the vine had taken it, and the forest had made of the taking a kind of slow and patient theft that no court would ever name.

"The post belongs to the path," Kael said quietly. "And the path belongs to whoever walks it."

"Then we walk it," Aria said, and closed her journal.

The vine above them shivered, and every tendril turned, as one, toward the dark mouth of the trail beyond the clearing.
