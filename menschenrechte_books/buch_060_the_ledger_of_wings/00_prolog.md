# The Ledger of Wings

The Roost clung to the cliff like a memory of stone, its walls built by beaks long dust, its ledges worn smooth by generations of restless feet. From the valley below, the aviary looked peaceful—a honeycomb of nests and archways catching the morning light. From within, it was a place where breath itself seemed to require permission.

The Keeper's ledger sat upon a stone pedestal in the central courtyard, its pages thick with columns of ink. Every seed that entered the Roost was counted there. Every feather that fell was noted. Every word spoken aloud was weighed, and words that stirred the air too boldly were marked in red—debt, the ledger called them, though every bird knew what red meant.

Corvus, the eldest Watcher, patrolled the upper ledges with his sharp black eyes and his sharper silences. He had learned long ago that survival meant seeing everything and saying nothing, and the habit had settled into his bones like frost. Yet each night he dreamed of the open sky beyond the cliff, and each morning he woke to the same walls.

Lumen, young and bright-feathered, had not yet learned such discipline. She sang when she was happy, and she was often happy, which made her dangerous. The Watchers had marked her twice already, and she wore her debt like a badge she did not understand.

Old Marta kept the water channel, measuring each drop that passed through the narrow stone gutter. She had grown thin in the years of rationing, her voice a whisper, but her eyes missed nothing. She remembered when the channel had run freely, and she never spoke of it—but her silence had a shape, and the shape was grief.

Pip, the smallest, ran errands between the ledges, carrying messages no one admitted to sending. He knew every crack in the crumbling cliff face, every loose stone, every gap where the wall had begun to fail. He told no one what he saw, but he watched the cracks grow wider each season.

Silas, chief among the Debtors, had once sung loudly enough to shake the rafters. Now he sat in the shadow of the ledger and counted his breaths, for each one was written against him. His feathers had dulled, and his eyes had learned to look at nothing.

The cliff face groaned in the night. A stone fell from the eastern wall and shattered on the courtyard stones. No one rose to examine it. The ledger recorded the loss—one stone, unaccounted—and the colony slept uneasily, dreaming of the day the walls would remember they were made of earth, and the earth would remember it could fall.

*When the counted silence grows louder than the crumbling stone, the cage that keeps the flock alive becomes the weight that pulls it down.*
