# The Ledger of Small Mercies

I was mending a net on the Third Landing when the bell gave its first stroke, and Old Tibbs said what he always said: "The tide keeps no secrets, but it keeps a schedule." He spat into the water and watched it go. That was his way — he tested every saying against the river before he trusted it.

The drowned man's ledger came up in the silt below the fish gates, bound in oilcloth and weighed with a brass clasp. Sable found it, because Sable finds everything the water gives back. She has one eye that wanders and one that does not, and she reads with the steady one. "Debts," she said, turning pages. "Favors. Names on every tier."

Deacon Ruel came down from the Bell Court with his hands folded. "A ledger is a promise kept in ink," he said. "The dead do not release us." He was a man who quoted proverbs the way other men draw breath, and he had never once lost a case at the Court. His conflict was simple: he believed the law was the town's spine, and he was not wrong, and that was the trouble.

The heirs came before the next flood. They stood on the Second Landing with the ledger held high and claimed the right to collect — every debt, every favor, every small mercy owed to a drowned man. Nissa of the Fifth Landing, who is twelve and quick and has never learned to hold her tongue, said: "A debt is a rope. You can pull with it or you can hang with it." The Court laughed. Then it stopped laughing.

Because the ledger listed her mother's name. And mine. And half the lower tiers'.

Maren Voss, who keeps the gate bell and has kept it for thirty years, rang the closing stroke early that evening. "The tide waits for no argument," she said, and her voice was flat as a wet stone. She has a habit of touching the bell rope before she speaks, as if testing whether the words will hold. Her conflict is the oldest one: she must close the gates on people she knows, twice a day, and she has never once been late, and she has never once been forgiven for it.

The threat is plain enough. The heirs hold the ledger. The Court holds the proverbs. And the lower landings hold the people whose names are written in a dead man's hand, waiting for the next bell to see whether the water or the debt will reach them first.

So I will set down what I know, the way Tibbs taught me — saying first, then the case, then the truth the river leaves behind.

*"A small mercy is a seed; the ungrateful man eats the harvest and calls it his own."*

The heirs recite it and demand their due. Nissa answers: "Then the seed was planted in my mother's yard, and the harvest is hers." The Court has not ruled.

Entry the First: the ledger is entered into the Bell Court. The gates open at dawn. The debt stands. The water does not care.
