# The Salt Tribunal

The North Sea did not care that fourteen days remained. It hurled itself against the pylons of Platform Meridian with the indifference of water that had never been asked to negotiate. Rain streaked the observation glass of Tribunal Chamber One—formerly the drilling crew's mess hall—where five people sat beneath a ceiling that had once held pipe fittings and now held broadcast cameras.

Judge Okonkwo had not slept. The rationed coffee had run out on day three, and she had taken to holding a cold tin cup during sessions, a habit the cameras had learned to love and she had learned to ignore. "Session four," she said, and her voice carried the flat patience of a woman who had buried two husbands and expected to bury this tribunal too. "Counsel for the displaced. Continue."

Maren Voss rose. She was forty-one and looked fifty, and she had stopped apologizing for it. "The tribunal has heard that the desalination plant's membrane stacks failed without warning. I call Tomas Reiter."

Tomas Reiter did not stand. He sat in the witness box with his hands flat on his knees, a maintenance engineer who had spent nineteen years inside the plant and now could not stop counting exits. "The warning system was offline," he said. "Eleven days. I filed it. I filed it twice."

"To whom?"

"To Petra Halvorsen's office."

At the respondent's table, Petra Halvorsen did not flinch. She was the corporation's counsel and she wore her composure like a coat she had paid for. Her tell was the pen—she clicked it once, softly, before every objection. "Objection. Relevance. The witness's filing habits are not before this tribunal."

"Overruled," Okonkwo said. "The filing is the tribunal's business."

Ilyas Demir leaned forward from the assessor's bench. He was the youngest of them and the only one who had grown up in the flooded district; he carried a photograph in his breast pocket that he had never shown the cameras, and he had a habit of asking the question everyone had agreed not to ask. "Mr. Reiter. When you filed the second report—did you believe people would die?"

Reiter's jaw worked. "I believed someone would read it."

The storm struck the platform then, a shudder that ran through the floor and rattled the water glasses. On the broadcast feed, the public watched the lights flicker. In the gallery, three displaced residents from the coastal city clutched each other. Somewhere below deck, the fuel gauge dropped another increment—fuel that Halvorsen's corporation had supplied, fuel that could be withdrawn.

Halvorsen clicked her pen. "The tribunal is reminded that its communications uplink is a courtesy of my client. Perhaps we might proceed before the courtesy expires."

Okonkwo set down her tin cup. "The tribunal will proceed at the tribunal's pace. Session four is adjourned until oh-eight-hundred."

The cameras kept running as the room emptied. They always did.
